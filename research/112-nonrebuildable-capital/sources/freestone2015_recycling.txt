Y ITS VERY NATURE, glass is eminently

suited for recycling. It is fragile, and it
easily breaks into fragments that are no
longer useful to the consumer but can be readily remelted so that the craftsman can reshape
the glass into new objects. It is not surprising,
then, that the recycling of glass is attested in the
Roman literature, as is indicated in the wellknown passages in the writings of Martial and
Statius.1 Archaeologically, recycling is more dif­
ficult to detect, and it has been noted in the context of metals that a successful recycling economy will be invisible because the recycled material
will have disappeared back into the crucible.2
Even so, sufficient archaeological evidence has
been recovered—in the form of caches of broken
glass from workshops, churches, and domestic
dwellings—to convincingly demonstrate that the
recycling of glass was widespread. In his review
of this material, Daniel Keller has observed that
recycling was probably not a uniform process;
instead, the approach taken would have been
very dependent upon context.3 For example, recycling by the Roman army is likely to have differed from that by a family of middling income
in the way that the glass was collected, stored,
and returned to the glassworkers. The glass recycling economy probably comprised a complex
network of individual consumers, institutions,
middlemen, and producers that offered a wide
range of pathways for a broken item to find its
way back into the melting pot.
Unfortunately, we are still far from fully understanding the details of recycling in any historical period. For Roman glass, a number of
fundamental questions remain unanswered: (1)
How efficient was the recycling process? Was

all glass recycled, and to what extent did recycled glass, as opposed to raw glass, satisfy the
needs of the Roman glass industry? (2) What
happened as Rome withdrew from peripheral
regions and the western empire collapsed? Did
glassworkers obtain new material from the east,
or were they dependent upon a diminishing reservoir of Roman material? (3) To what extent
was Roman glass reused in the medieval period
and beyond, as suggested by the writings of
Theo­philus and Eraclius?
This article addresses these issues through the
examination of chemical analytical data. Glass
chemistry is sensitive to recycling in several
ways. Glass compositions are not homogeneous,
but vary with time and location of production.
The mixing of two different types of glass will
produce intermediate compositions that can be
recognized graphically because they lie directly
between the two original “end-members.” Furthermore, the separation of glass colors in recycling processes is rarely perfect. A few fragments

Another effect of recycling is more subtle, but
in some cases may be detected. This is the deterioration in composition caused by (a) the incorporation of dirt and dust from the environment,
and deposits on the glass itself; (b) the loss of
alkali fluxes, particularly sodium, as the glass is
progressively reheated; (c) the incorporation of
contaminants from the equipment of the glass
workshop, notably iron, which will have scaled
off from the blowpipe and pontil, and been absorbed from the wall of the clay melting pot;
and (d) the incorporation of elements derived
from the fuel ash, notably potassium, magnesium, and phosphorus. These processes can com­
bine to cause a deterioration in the color of the
glass, as well as an increase in viscosity (stiffness)
of the molten glass, rendering it more difficult

to work. Although not dramatic, these gradual
processes will eventually render the glass unusable unless fresh glass is added to the batch.
A final characteristic of recycling is that, if it
continues over a period when there is a change
in composition of primary or newly made glass,
it can produce compositions that are anachronistic, as will be seen.

Recycling in the Roman Period
Specialists working on the blown transparent
glass of the first to third centuries A.D. typical­ly
classify it as either “colorless” or “weakly colored,” the latter term referring to transparent
glass that tends toward colorless but retains a
blue or green tinge. Most Roman glass has much
higher concentrations of iron oxide than modern container or window glass,5 and would nat­
urally be weakly colored were it not for the addi­
tion of decolorizers by the glassmakers. It is well
understood that the Romans used two types
of decolorizers: manganese dioxide (MnO2) and
an­
timony pentoxide (Sb2O5).6 In addition to
glasses that have added manganese or antimony, there is a third category, containing both of
these components. Glass containing only antimony and no manganese tends to be colorless,
glass containing manganese in excess of one percent tends to be colorless, glass with lower manganese tends to be green-blue, and glass containing both manganese and antimony tends to

be green-blue. But these relationships are certain­
ly not invariable because the effectiveness of the
decolorization depends upon factors besides the
absolute concentration of the decolorizer.
Recent work increasingly suggests that manganese- and antimony-based decolorizers were
added to the glass at the primary glassmak­ing stage: when the glass was made from sand
and alkali. For example, analysis of glass from
Roman-period sites around the Wadi Natrun
shows the presence of manganese or antimony
in the primary glass.7 There is no evidence of
the addition of both manganese and antimony
to the same batch at the primary stage, however.
Recent work on glass chunks from shipwrecks
around the Bay of Haifa indicates the presence
of raw glass with either antimony or manganese, but no mixed glass,8 and work on Roman
glass vessels from Egypt seems to show an abundance of antimony-decolorized glass, but relatively lim­ited amounts of manganese or mixed
manganese-antimony glass.9 Caroline Jackson
and others have observed that a potential expla­
nation for the occurrence of manganese and antimony together in Roman glass is that manganese- and antimony-decolorized glasses were
mixed through recycling, a view that is consistent with the evidence from the eastern Mediter­
ranean.10
The effects and extent of the recycling proc­
ess can be seen by close examination of one of
the few large sets of data from the Roman period that include analyses from a single archaeological assemblage of both antimony- and

manganese-decolorized glasses, together with
green-blue glasses. This assemblage is the glass
in the cargo of the Iulia Felix, a ship that sank
in the Adriatic in the second or third century.
The Iulia Felix was carrying a barrel of about
140 kilograms of broken vessel glass for re­
cycling, and the composition of this glass has
been comprehensively investigated by Alberta
Silvestri and colleagues, who note that a signif­
icant proportion of the glass cargo shows evidence of previous recycling.11 In this assemblage,
blue-green glass greatly exceeds colorless glass
in quantity and most of the colorless glass is
antimony-decolorized only, with a small quantity of manganese-colorless glass.

of all of the glasses, labeled as to whether they
con­­

probably made in different workshops using

would mix the glass types completely, so that

However, this does not appear to have been the
case in the Roman period, no doubt in part because the glass supply was frequently refreshed
by newly made glass from the primary suppliers.
We have been able to identify fresh glass in
the Iulia Felix assemblage because the glass being employed in the area of embarkation (presumably Italy) was from two primary sources
that had distinctive bulk compositions. When
the glass used originates from a single primary
source, such as at Bubastis in Egypt, where most
of the glass falls into the “antimony-colorless”
category,13 we cannot be sure that there was a
substantial recycling process because we cannot
detect compositional changes due to mixing.
The distribution of compositions in Figure
2 is informative in another way. There appears
to be a large amount of manganese-decolorized
glass with small amounts of antimony-decolorized glass added, suggesting that it was common
practice to recycle manganese-decolorized glass.
However, casual adulteration of antimonydecolorized glass appears to have been less common. This suggests that antimony-decolorized
glass was conserved more carefully than manganese-decolorized glass. Such a practice would
not be surprising because measurements of the
oxidation states of iron indicate that antimony

was a more effective decolorizer than manganese, and that antimony-decolorized glass was
more oxidized.14 Furthermore, ores of antimony
are less abundant than those of manganese, and
we might expect the antimony-decolorized glass
to have been more expensive in terms of raw
materials.
The types of glass in use and the extent of
recycling were dependent upon location. The
overwhelming abundance of antimony-decolor­
ized glass on an Egyptian site such as Bubastis
may suggest that this is a primary glass type that
was made in Egypt, perhaps in the region of
Alex­andria. The relatively high soda content
(see Figure 1) also suggests that this glass type
was made close to the Egyptian natron sources.
This would be consistent with the price edict of

The withdrawal of the Roman army from
the western empire in the fifth century led to a
marked reduction in the use of glass vessels,20
and east–west trade was dramatically curtailed
throughout the rest of the first millennium.21
Although a substantial production of raw glass
seems to have continued in Palestine and Egypt,22
the quantities of fresh material that reached the
northwest seem to have been relatively limited.

This is likely to have been driven in part by the
expansion of the enameling industry in Limoges

how the tesserae to be used had to be tested (to
exclude tesserae made of ceramic or stone) and
cleaned, probably to remove mortar and weathering products.39 Freshly made glass would have
been very desirable.
It is noteworthy that the change from the use
of Roman tesserae to freshly made glass in
enameling occurred at about the same time (in
the years around 1200) that the use of Roman
tesserae to color medieval window glass ceased.
Presumably, the supply of old opaque colored
glass would have been difficult to sustain, particularly as demand in northwestern Europe
grew.
Not only colored and opaque Roman glass
was reused in late medieval times. The late 12thcentury head reliquary of Saint Eustace (Fig. 6),
for example, contains colorless cabochons that
appear to be Roman glass40 and may reflect the
procedure outlined in the text ascribed to Era­
cli­us, whereby molten Roman glass was pressed
into a clay mold to make “beautiful shining
gems.”41

Glass employed in the manufacture of windows and mosaics in the first to third centuries
appears to have been an important resource for
later generations, and it is remarkable that the
recycling of old Roman material made a significant and sometimes very substantial contribution to the northern European glass industry
from the eighth to 12th centuries. The fact that
it is still being exploited for commercial purposes, in the form of jewelry production, is even
more remarkable, and a reflection of the vast
quantities that must have been made at the
height of the Roman period.
This article has presented an outline of the
recycling of Roman glass, as understood through
its chemistry. There is still much to learn, particularly in terms of the different social and com­
mercial processes through which recycling was
promoted, the types of environment in which it
thrived, and the efficiency of the recycling proc­
ess. Even so, it appears that recycling was ubiquitous and that old glass was exploited in every
possible way.
In writing this, I have been inspired by the
work of David Whitehouse, who was able to
move from the particular to the big picture in
such an elegant and seamless way. I doubt I have
come anywhere near to producing a piece of
work that approaches his high scholarly standard. But I know that, however it has turned
out, David would have discussed it with the kind
and generous interest that he always showed
when dealing with the contributions of others.
As both a scholar and a person, David set the
bar high, and he has left something to which all
of us can aspire.
