{{short description|Societal collapse in the Late Bronze Age}}
{{Use dmy dates|date=May 2019}}
[[File:Bronze Age End.svg|thumb|upright=1.35|Invasions, destruction and population movements during the Late Bronze Age collapse]]
{{Bronze Age}}
The '''Late Bronze Age collapse''' was a period of [[societal collapse]] in the [[Mediterranean basin]] during the late 13th to early 12th century&nbsp;BC. It is thought to have affected much of the [[Eastern Mediterranean]] and [[Near East]], in particular [[History of Egypt|Egypt]], [[Anatolia]], the [[Aegean Sea#History|Aegean]], [[ancient Libya|eastern Libya]], and the [[Balkans]]. The collapse was sudden, violent, and culturally disruptive for many [[Bronze Age]] civilizations, creating a sharp material decline for the region's previously existing powers.

The [[palace economy]] of [[Mycenaean Greece]], the [[Aegean civilization|Aegean region]], and [[History of Anatolia|Anatolia]] that characterized the Late Bronze Age broke apart into the small isolated village cultures of the [[Greek Dark Ages]], which lasted from {{circa|1200 BC}} to {{circa|800&nbsp;BC|lk=no}} and were followed by the better‑known [[Archaic Greece|Archaic Age]]. The [[Hittite Empire]] spanning Anatolia and the [[Levant]] collapsed, while states such as the [[Middle Assyrian Empire]] in [[Mesopotamia]] and the [[New Kingdom of Egypt]] survived in weakened forms. Other cultures, such as the [[Phoenicians]], enjoyed increased autonomy and power with the waning military presence of Egypt and Assyria in [[West Asia]].

Competing theories of the cause of the Late Bronze Age collapse have been proposed since the 19th century, with most involving the violent destruction of cities and towns. These include [[Climate variability and change|climate change]], volcanic eruptions, droughts, disease, invasions by the [[Sea Peoples]], economic disruptions due to increased [[ironworking]], and changes in [[military technology]] and strategy that brought the decline of [[chariot]] warfare. Following the collapse, gradual changes in [[ferrous metallurgy|metallurgic technology]] led to the subsequent [[Iron Age]] across Europe, Asia, and Africa during the 1st millennium&nbsp;BC. Scholarship in the late 20th and early 21st century introduced views that the collapse was more limited in scale and scope than previously thought.<ref name="auto">{{cite book |last=Millek |first=Jesse |title=Destruction and Its Impact on Ancient Societies at the End of the Bronze Age |publisher=Lockwood Press |location=Columbus, Georgia, US |date=2023 |isbn=978-1-948488-83-9}}</ref><ref name="auto2">{{Cite journal |title=Getting closer to the Late Bronze Age collapse in the Aegean and eastern Mediterranean, c. 1200 BC |first=Guy D. |last=Middleton |date=25 February 2024 |journal=Antiquity |volume=98 |issue=397 |pages=260–263 |via=[[Cambridge University Press]] |doi=10.15184/aqy.2023.187 |doi-access=free}} This is a detailed academic review of {{harvp|Millek|2023}}.</ref><ref name="auto3">{{cite book |last1=Jung |first1=Reinhard |last2=Kardamaki |first2=Eleftheria |title=Synchronizing the Destructions of the Mycenaean Palaces |publisher=Austrian Academy of Sciences Press |location=Vienna |date=2022 |isbn=978-3-7001-8877-3}}</ref>

==Scholarship==
{{Trojan War}}
The German historian [[Arnold Hermann Ludwig Heeren]] first dated the Late Bronze Age collapse to 1200&nbsp;BC. In an 1817 history of Ancient Greece, Heeren stated that the first period of Greek prehistory ended around this time, based on a dating of the [[fall of Troy]] to 1190&nbsp;BC. In 1826, he dated the end of the [[Nineteenth Dynasty of Egypt]] to around the same time. Also occurring in the first half of the 12th century BC were the [[Sea Peoples]] invasions, the collapse of [[Mycenaean Greece]] and the [[Kassites]] in [[Babylonia]], and the carving of the [[Merneptah Stele]]. This period also saw the destruction of [[Ugarit]] and the [[Amorites|Amorite states]] in the [[Levant]], the fragmentation of western [[Anatolia]]'s [[Luwians|Luwian states]], and chaos in [[Canaan]].<ref>S. Richard, "Archaeological sources for the history of Palestine: The Early Bronze Age: The rise and collapse of urbanism", ''The Biblical Archaeologist'' (1987)</ref> The deterioration of these governments interrupted trade routes and led to severely reduced literacy rates in much of this area.<ref name="Cultural">{{cite encyclopedia |first1=Russ |last1=Crawford |editor1-last=Stanton |editor1-first=Andrea |editor2-last=Ramsay |editor2-first=Edward |editor3-last=Seybolt |editor3-first=Peter J. |editor4-last=Elliott |editor4-first=Carolyn |title=Chronology |encyclopedia=Cultural Sociology of the Middle East, Asia, and Africa: An Encyclopedia |date=2006 |publisher=Sage |isbn=978-1-4129-8176-7 |page=xxix |url= https://books.google.com/books?id=GtCL2OYsH6wC&pg=PR29 |access-date=21 May 2020}}</ref>

=== Debate ===
Initially, historians believed that in the first phase of this period, almost every city between [[Pylos]] and [[Gaza City|Gaza]] was violently destroyed, and many were abandoned, including [[Hattusa]], [[Mycenae]], and [[Ugarit]], with [[Robert Drews]] claiming that, "Within a period of forty to fifty years at the end of the thirteenth and the beginning of the twelfth century, almost every significant city in the eastern Mediterranean world was destroyed, many of them never to be occupied again."<ref>The physical destruction of palaces and cities is the subject of Robert Drews's ''The End of the Bronze Age: changes in warfare and the catastrophe ca. 1200 B.C.'', 1993.</ref>

However, more recent research has shown that Drews overestimated the number of destroyed cities and referenced destructions that never happened. According to ancient historian and archaeologist Jesse Millek:<ref>{{cite news |last1=Millek |first1=Jesse |title=The Fall of the Bronze Age and the Destruction that Wasn't: The Ancient Near East Today |url= https://anetoday.org/millek-fall-bronze-age-destruction/ |work=ANE Today |date=20 December 2022 |ref={{sfnref|Millek|2022a}} }}</ref>

{{blockquote|If one goes through archaeological literature from the past 150 years, there are 148 sites with 153 destruction events ascribed to the end of the Late Bronze Age ca. 1200 BC. However, of these, 94, or 61%, have either been misdated, assumed based on little evidence, or simply never happened at all. For Drews's map, and his subsequent discussion of some other sites which he believed were destroyed ca. 1200 BC, of the 60 "destructions" 31, or 52%, are false destructions. The complete list of false destructions includes other notable sites such as: Lefkandi, Orchomenos, Athens, Knossos, Alassa, Carchemish, Aleppo, Alalakh, Hama, Qatna, Kadesh, Tell Tweini, Byblos, Tyre, Sidon, Ashdod, Ashkelon, Beth-Shean, Tell Dier Alla, and many more.}}

Ann Killebrew has demonstrated that cities such as [[Jerusalem]] functioned as important walled settlements during the West Asian Middle Bronze IIB and Iron Age IIC [[Bronze Age#Periodization|periods]] ({{circa|1800–1550}} and {{circa|720–586}} BC), yet in the intervening Late Bronze (LB) and Iron Age I and IIA/B periods, sites like Jerusalem remained small, relatively insignificant, and unfortified.<ref>{{cite book |editor1-last=Killebrew |editor1-first=Ann E. |chapter=Biblical Jerusalem: An Archaeological Assessment |editor2-first=Andrew G. |editor2-last=Vaughn |first=Ann E. |last=Killebrew |title=Jerusalem in Bible and Archaeology: The First Temple Period |publisher=Society of Biblical Literature |date=2003 |isbn=978-1-58983-066-0 |url= https://books.google.com/books?id=yYS4VEu08h4C |access-date=9 October 2018}}</ref> Some recent writing argues that although some collapses may have happened in this period, these may not have been widespread.<ref name="auto2" /><ref name="auto" /><ref name="auto3" />

== Background ==
Advanced civilizations with extensive trade networks and complex sociopolitical institutions characterized the Late Bronze Age ({{circa|1550}}–1200 BC).<ref name=":6">{{Cite journal |last=Orphanides |first=Andreas G. |date=January 2017 |title=Late Bronze Age Socio-Economic and Political Organization, and the Hellenization of Cyprus |journal=Athens Journal of History |volume=3 |issue=1 |pages=7–20 |doi=10.30958/ajhis.3-1-1 |doi-access=free}}</ref> Prominent societies (Egyptians, Hittites, Mesopotamians, and Mycenaeans) exhibited monumental architecture, advanced metallurgy, and literacy.<ref name=":5">{{Cite web |last=Mark |first=Joshua J. |date=20 September 2019 |title=Bronze Age Collapse |url= https://www.worldhistory.org/Bronze_Age_Collapse/ |access-date=2 August 2024 |website=World History Encyclopedia}}</ref> Flourishing trade in copper, timber, pottery, and agricultural goods,<ref name=":6" /> as well as diplomatic ties progressively deepened their interdependence.<ref name=":5" /> Geopolitical powers of the time relied on variations of the palace economy system, in which wealth is first concentrated in a centralized bureaucracy before being redistributed according to the sovereign's agenda,<ref>{{cite book |last1=de Blois |first1=Lukas |last2=van der Spek |first2=R. J. |translator-last=Mellor |translator-first=Susan |title=An Introduction to the Ancient World |publisher=Routledge |date=1997 |isbn=0-415-12773-4 |pages=56–60}}</ref> a system which primarily benefits the society's elite.<ref name=":6" /> This intricate web of dependencies, coupled with the inflexibility of the palace system, exposed these civilizations to the cascading effects of distant disturbances.{{sfnp|Cline|2014|p=165}}

==Evidence==
=== Anatolia ===
[[File:Hittites 1300bc.jpg|thumb|The [[Hittites|Hittite Empire]] around 1300&nbsp;BC]]
[[Image:Sphinx_Gate,_Hattusa_01.jpg|right|thumb|Ruins of the Sphinx Gate entrance to the [[Hittite Empire|Hittite]] city of [[Hattusa]]]]

Many Anatolian sites were destroyed at the Late Bronze Age, and the area appears to have undergone extreme political decentralization. For much of the Late Bronze Age, Anatolia had been dominated by the [[Hittites|Hittite Empire]], but by 1200&nbsp;BC, the state was already fragmenting under the strain of famine, plague, and civil war.{{citation needed|date=July 2024|reason=Nothing hugely controversial here, leaving this tag to make it clear that the preceding sentences aren't covered by the Seeher citation.}} The Hittite capital of [[Hattusa]] was burned at an unknown date in this general period, though it may in fact have been abandoned at that point.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Seeher |first=Jürgen |date=2001 |title=Die Zerstörung der Stadt Hattusa, in: G. Wilhelm (Hrsg.), Akten des IV. Internationalen Kongresses für Hethitologie. Studien zu den Boğazköy-Texten 45 (Wiesbaden 2001) 623–634 |journal=Akten des IV. Internationalen Kongresses für Hethitologie. Studien zu den Boğazköy-Texten 45 |url= https://www.academia.edu/23031747 |via=Academia.edu |access-date=5 November 2022}}</ref><ref>{{Cite book |last=Seeher |first=Jürgen |date=2010 |contribution=After the Empire: Observations on the Early Iron Age in Central Anatolia |editor-last=Singer |editor-first1=Itamar |title=Ipamati Kistamati Pari Tumatimis: Luwian and Hittite Studies Presented to J. David Hawkins on the Occasion of his 70th Birthday |series="Emery and Claire Yass Publications in Archaeology" ser. |volume=28 |publisher=Institute of Archaeology, [[Tel Aviv University]] |page=221 |isbn=978-965-266-026-8 |url= https://www.academia.edu/16780675 |via=Academia.edu |access-date=5 November 2022}}</ref> Karaoğlan,{{efn|The name Karaoğlan is [[Turkish language|Turkish]]; the original Hittite name is unknown.<ref>Robbins, p. 170</ref>}} near present-day [[Ankara]], was burned and the corpses left unburied.<ref name="Drews1995">{{cite book |last=Drews |first=Robert |url= https://books.google.com/books?id=bFpK6aXEWN8C&pg=PA8 |title=The End of the Bronze Age: Changes in Warfare and the Catastrophe Ca. 1200 B.C. |publisher=[[Princeton University Press]] |date=1995 |isbn=978-0-691-02591-9 |page=8 |access-date=9 December 2016}}</ref> Many Anatolian sites have destruction layers dating to this general period. Some of them such as [[Troy]] were immediately rebuilt, while others such as [[Kaymakçı (archaeological site)|Kaymakçı]] were abandoned.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://gygaia.org/excavations-at-kaymakci/|title=Excavations at Kaymakçı – Gygaia Projects|access-date=September 5, 2026}}</ref>

This period appears to have also been a time of migration. For instance, some evidence suggests that the [[Phrygians]] arrived in Anatolia during this period, possibly through the [[Bosporus]] or over the Caucasus Mountains.<ref>Bryce, Trevor. ''The Kingdom of the Hittites''. (Clarendon), p. 379</ref>

Initially, the Assyrian Empire maintained a presence in the area. However, it gradually withdrew from much of the region for a time in the second half of the 11th century BC.{{citation needed|date=July 2024}}

=== Cyprus ===
During the reign of the [[Tudḫaliya IV]] (reigned c. 1237–1209&nbsp;BC), the island was briefly invaded by the Hittites, either to secure the [[copper]] resource or as a way of preventing [[piracy]]. Shortly afterwards, the island was reconquered by his son [[Suppiluliuma II]] around 1200&nbsp;BC.<ref>Bryce, Trevor. ''The Kingdom of the Hittites'' (Clarendon), p. 366.</ref>

There is little evidence of destruction on the island of Cyprus in the years surrounding 1200&nbsp;BC which marks the separation between the [[Prehistoric Cyprus|Late Cypriot II]] (LCII) from the LCIII period.<ref name=":1">{{Cite book |last=Georgiou |first=Artemis |contribution=Flourishing amidst a 'Crisis': The regional history of the Paphos polity during the transition from the 13th to the 12th centuries BCE |editor1-last=Fischer |editor1-first=Peter M. |editor2-last=Bürge |editor2-first=Teresa |title="Sea Peoples" Up-to-Date: New Research on Transformations in the Eastern Mediterranean in the 13th−11th Centuries BCE |publisher=Austrian Academy of Sciences |location=Vienna |date=2017 |orig-date=3–4 November 2014 |isbn=978-3-7001-7963-4 |chapter-url= https://www.academia.edu/33330863 |via=Academia.edu |access-date=4 November 2022}}</ref> The city of [[Kition]] is commonly cited as destroyed at the end of the LC IIC, but the excavator, Vassos Karageorghis, made it expressly clear that it was not destroyed, stating, "At Kition, major rebuilding was carried out in both excavated Areas&nbsp;I and II, but there is no evidence of violent destruction; on the contrary, we observe a cultural continuity."<ref>{{Cite book |last=Karageorghis |first=Vassos |contribution=The Crisis Years: Cyprus |editor1-first=William A. |editor1-last=Ward |editor2-first=Martha |editor2-last=Sharp Joukowsky |title=The Crisis Years: The 12th Century B.C. from Beyond the Danube to the Tigris |location=Dubuque, Iowa |publisher=Kendall/Hunt |pages=79–86 |date=1992 |isbn=978-0-8403-7148-5}}</ref> Jesse Millek has demonstrated that while it is possible that the city of Enkomi was destroyed, the archaeological evidence is not clear. Both Enkomi buildings from the end of LC IIC showed limited burning, with most rooms undamaged.{{sfnp|Millek|2021b|pp=74–77}} The same can be said for the site of Sinda as it is not clear if it was destroyed since only some ash was found but no other evidence that the city was destroyed like fallen walls or burnt rubble.{{sfnp|Millek|2021b|p=76}} The only settlement on Cyprus that has clear evidence it was destroyed around 1200&nbsp;BC was [[Maa Palaeokastro]], which was likely destroyed by some sort of attack,{{sfnp|Millek|2021b|pp=79–80}} though the excavators were not sure who attacked it, saying, "We might suggest that [the attackers] were 'pirates', 'adventurers' or remnants of the 'Sea Peoples', but this is simply another way of saying that we do not know."<ref>{{Cite book |first=Vassos |last=Karageorghis |title=Excavations at Maa-Palaeokastro 1979–1986 |date=1988 |publisher=Cyprus Department of Antiquities |isbn=9963-36-409-8 |page=266 |oclc=19842786 }}</ref>

Several settlements on Cyprus were abandoned at the end of the LC IIC or during the first half of the 12th century BC without destruction such as Pyla [[Kokkinokremmos]], Toumba tou Skourou, Alassa, and Maroni-Vournes.<ref name=":1" /> While the Eastern Mediterranean declined, parts of Cyprus such as Kition and Paphos prospered after 1200 BC during the LC IIIA.<ref name=":1" /><ref>{{Cite journal |last=Iacovou |first=Maria |date=2008 |title=Cultural and Political Configurations in Iron Age Cyprus: The Sequel to a Protohistoric Episode |journal=American Journal of Archaeology |volume=112 |issue=4 |pages=625–657 |doi=10.3764/aja.112.4.625 |doi-access=free |jstor=20627513 |s2cid=55793645}}</ref>

=== Greece ===
{{Main|Greek Dark Ages}}
[[File:Large Krater with Armored Men Departing for Battle, Mycenae acropolis, 12th century BC (3402016857).jpg|thumb|Marching soldiers on the [[Warrior Vase]], {{Circa|1200 BC}}, a [[krater]] from Mycenae]]

Destruction was heaviest at palaces and fortified sites, and none of the Mycenaean palaces of the Late Bronze Age survived (with the possible exception of the [[Cyclopean]] fortifications on the [[Acropolis of Athens]]). Thebes was one of the earliest examples of this, having its palace sacked repeatedly between 1300 and 1200&nbsp;BC and eventually completely destroyed by fire. The extent of this destruction is highlighted by Robert Drews, who reasons that the destruction was such that Thebes did not resume a significant position in Greece until at least the late 12th century&nbsp;BC.{{sfnp|Drews|1993|p=22}} Many other sites offer less conclusive causes; for example, it is unclear what happened at Athens, although it is clear that the settlement saw a significant decline during the Bronze Age Collapse. While there is no evidence of remnants of a destroyed palace or central structure, a change in location of living quarters and burial sites demonstrates a significant recession.{{sfnp|Drews|1993|p=25}} Furthermore, the increase in fortification at this site suggests much fear of the decline in Athens. [[Vincent Desborough]] asserts that this is evidence of later migrations away from the city in reaction to its initial decline, although a significant population did remain.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Desborough |first=Vincent R. d'A. |author-link=Vincent Desborough |title=The last Mycenaeans and their successors; an archaeological survey, c. 1200 – c. 1000 B.C. |publisher=[[Clarendon Press]] |date=1964 |location=Oxford |page=113}}</ref> It remains possible that this emigration from Athens was not flight from violence. Nancy Demand posits that environmental changes could have played an important role in the collapse of Athens. In particular, Demand notes the presence of "enclosed and protected means of access to water sources at Athens" as evidence of persistent droughts in the region that could have resulted in a fragile reliance on imports.<ref name="Demand, Nancy H. 2011 198">{{Cite book |last=Demand |first=Nancy H. |title=The Mediterranean Context of Early Greek History |date=2011 |publisher=[[Wiley-Blackwell]] |isbn=978-1-4443-4233-8 |location=Chichester |page=198 |oclc=823737347}}</ref>

[[File:Tíryns.jpg|thumb|View of the Megaron of the palace at [[Tiryns]], one of the many Greek palaces destroyed during the Bronze Age Collapse]]
Up to 90% of small sites in the [[Peloponnese]] were abandoned, suggesting a major depopulation. The extent of this destruction remains unclear, as at many other Greek sites. The city of Mycenae, for example, was initially destroyed in an earthquake in 1250&nbsp;BC as evidenced by the presence of crushed bodies buried in collapsed buildings.<ref name="Demand, Nancy H. 2011 198" /> However, the site was rebuilt only to face destruction in 1190&nbsp;BC as the result of a series of major fires. There is a suggestion by Robert Drews that the fires could have been the result of an attack on the site and its palace; however, [[Eric H. Cline|Eric Cline]] points out the lack of archaeological evidence for an attack.{{sfnp|Drews|1993|p=23}}{{sfnp|Cline|2014|p=130}} Thus, while fire was definitely the cause of the destruction, it is unclear what or who caused it. A similar situation occurred at [[Tiryns]] in 1200&nbsp;BC, when an earthquake destroyed much of the city including its palace. It is likely however that the city continued to be inhabited for some time following the earthquake. As a result, there is a general agreement that earthquakes did not permanently destroy Mycenae or Tiryns because, as is highlighted by Guy Middleton, "Physical destruction then cannot fully explain the collapse".<ref name="middleton">{{Cite journal |last=Middleton |first=Guy D. |date=September 2012 |title=Nothing Lasts Forever: Environmental Discourses on the Collapse of Past Societies |journal=Journal of Archaeological Research |volume=20 |issue=3 |pages=257–307 |doi=10.1007/s10814-011-9054-1 |s2cid=144866495}}</ref> Drews points out that there was continued occupation at these sites, accompanied by attempts to rebuild, demonstrating the continuation of Tiryns as a settlement.{{sfnp|Drews|1993|p=25}} Demand proposes that the cause may once more have been environmental, chiefly a shortfall in domestic food production and the palaces' function in administering and stockpiling imported food, meaning their destruction would have merely aggravated the paramount issue of food scarcity.<ref name="Demand, Nancy H. 2011 198" /> The importance of trade as a factor is supported by {{Ill|Spyros Iakovidis|el|Σπύρος Ιακωβίδης}}, who points out the lack of evidence for violent or sudden decline in Mycenae.{{sfnp|Cline|2014|p=131}}

Pylos offers some more clues to its destruction, as the intensive and extensive destruction by fire around 1180&nbsp;BC reflects the violent destruction of the city.{{sfnp|Cline|2014|p=129}} There is some evidence of Pylos expecting a seaborne attack, with tablets at Pylos discussing "Watchers guarding the coast".<ref>{{Cite book |last=Ventris |first=Michael |title=Documents in Mycenaean Greek: three hundred selected tablets from Knossos, Plyos, and Mycenae with commentary and vocabulary |date=1959 |publisher=[[Cambridge University Press]] |page=189 |oclc=70408199}}</ref> Eric Cline rebuts the idea that this is evidence of an attack by Sea People, pointing out that the tablet does not say what is being watched for or why. Cline does not see naval attacks as playing a role in Pylos's decline.{{sfnp|Cline|2014|p=129}} Demand, however, argues that, regardless of what the threat from the sea was, it likely played a role in the decline, at least in hindering trade and perhaps vital food imports.{{sfnp|Demand|2011|p=199}}

The Bronze Age collapse marked the start of what has been called the [[Greek Dark Ages]], which lasted roughly 400 years and ended with the establishment of [[Archaic Greece]]. Other cities, such as [[Athens]], continued to be occupied, but with a more local sphere of influence, limited evidence of trade and an impoverished culture, from which it took centuries to recover.

These sites in Greece show evidence of the collapse: [[Knossos]], [[Kydonia]], [[Lefkandi]], [[Menelaion]], [[Mycenae]], [[Nichoria]], [[Pylos]], {{ill|Teichos Dymaion|el|Τείχος Δυμαίων}}, [[Thebes, Greece|Thebes]], [[Tiryns]], and [[Iolkos]]{{sfnp|Drews|1993}} .

=== Egypt ===
{{main|End of the 19th Dynasty}}
[[File:Egyptian Empire, B.C. 1450 (2710782990).jpg|thumb|upright=1|[[Egyptian Empire]] at 1450&nbsp;BC]]

While it survived the Bronze Age collapse, the Egyptian Empire of the [[New Kingdom of Egypt|New Kingdom era]] receded considerably in territorial and economic strength during the mid-twelfth century (during the reign of [[Ramesses VI]], 1145 to 1137 BC). Previously, the [[Merneptah Stele]] ({{Circa|1200 BC}}) spoke of attacks (Libyan War) from Putrians (from modern [[Libya]]), with associated people of [[Achaeans (Homer)|Ekwesh]], [[Sea Peoples|Shekelesh]], [[Lukka lands|Lukka]], [[Sherden|Shardana]] and Teresh (possibly an Egyptian name for the [[Tyrrhenians]] or [[Troy|Troas]]), and a Canaanite revolt, in the cities of [[Ashkelon]], [[Yenoam]] and among the [[Israelites|people of Israel]]. A second attack ([[Battle of the Delta]] and [[Battle of Djahy]]) during the reign of [[Ramesses III]] (1186–1155 BC) involved [[Peleset]], [[Tjeker]], Shardana, and [[Denyen]].

The Nubian War, the First Libyan War, the Northern War and the Second Libyan War were all victories for Ramesses, but the expense strained the Egyptian economy and nearly bankrupted the state treasury. Nevertheless, the defeat of the invading Sea Peoples, [[Libyans]], and [[Nubians]] secured Egypt's frontiers during the age of collapse. Ramesses III boasted: "My sword is great and mighty like that of [[Montu]]. No land can stand fast before my arms. I am a king rejoicing in slaughter. My reign is calmed in peace."<ref>{{Cite book |editor1-last=Edgerton |editor1-first=William F. |editor2-last=Wilson |editor2-first=John A. |title=Historical Records of Ramses III: The Texts in Medinet Habu Volumes 1 and 2 |series="Studies in Ancient Oriental Civilization" ser. |volume=12 |publisher=[[University of Chicago Press]] |date=1936 |url= https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/saoc12.pdf |url-status=live |access-date=4 May 2019 |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20190504185355/https://oi.uchicago.edu/sites/oi.uchicago.edu/files/uploads/shared/docs/saoc12.pdf |archive-date=4 May 2019}}</ref>

[[File:Medinet Habu Ramses III. Tempel Nordostwand Abzeichnung 01.jpg|thumb|[[Sea Peoples]] in their ships during the battle with the Egyptians. Relief from the mortuary temple of [[Ramesses III]] at [[Medinet Habu (temple)|Medinet Habu]]]]
[[File:East-Hem 1000bc.jpg|thumb|The main polities in Eurasia around 1000&nbsp;BC]] 
Egypt withdrew from the [[Southern Levant]] over about a hundred years, most likely due to the political turmoil in Egypt proper. Many Egyptian garrisons or sites with an "Egyptian governor's residence" in the Southern Levant were abandoned without destruction, including [[Deir al-Balah]], [[Ashkelon]], Tel Mor, [[Tell el-Far'ah (South)]], [[Tel Gerisa]], [[Tell Jemmeh]], [[Tel Masos]], and Qubūr al-Walaydah.<ref name=":0">{{Cite journal |last=Millek |first=Jesse Michael |date=2018 |title=Destruction and the Fall of Egyptian Hegemony Over the Southern Levant |journal=Journal of Ancient Egyptian Interconnections |language= |volume=19 |issue=1 |pages=11–15 |issn=1944-2815 |url= https://journals.librarypublishing.arizona.edu/jaei/article/id/1347/ |access-date=19 February 2025 |url-status=live |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20240611000901/https://journals.librarypublishing.arizona.edu/jaei/article/1347/galley/1342/download/ |archive-date=11 June 2024 |ref={{sfnref|Millek|2018a}} }}</ref> Not all Egyptian sites in the Southern Levant were abandoned peacefully. The Egyptian garrison at Aphek was destroyed, likely by warfare, at the end of the 13th century.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Millek |first=Jesse |contribution=Sea Peoples, Philistines, and the Destruction of Cities: A Critical Examination of Destruction Layers 'Caused' by the 'Sea Peoples' |editor1-last=Fischer |editor1-first=Peter |editor2-last=Bürge |editor2-first=Teresa |title="Sea Peoples" Up-to-Date: New Research on Transformation in the Eastern Mediterranean in 13th–11th Centuries BCE, 113–140 |publisher=Austrian Academy of Sciences Press |date=2017 |isbn=978-3-7001-7963-4 |pages=120–122 |jstor=j.ctt1v2xvsn}}</ref> The Egyptian gate complex uncovered at Jaffa was destroyed between 1134 and 1115&nbsp;BC based on C14 dates,<ref>{{Cite journal |title=Burke et al. 2017 Excavations of the New Kingdom Fortress in Jaffa, 2011–2014: Traces of Resistance to Egyptian Rule in Canaan |journal=American Journal of Archaeology |volume=121 |issue=1 |date=January 2017 |pages=85–133 |doi=10.3764/aja.121.1.0085 |doi-access=free}}</ref> while [[Beit She'an]] was partially destroyed, possibly by an earthquake, in the mid-12th century.<ref name=":0" />

=== The Levant ===
{{see also|Canaan}}
Egyptian evidence shows that from the reign of [[Horemheb]] (ruled either 1319 or 1306 to 1292&nbsp;BC), wandering [[Shasu]] were more problematic than the earlier [[ʿApiru]]. [[Ramesses II]] (r. 1279–1213&nbsp;BC) campaigned against them, pursuing them as far as [[Moab]], where he established a fortress after a near defeat at the [[Battle of Kadesh]]. During the reign of [[Merneptah]], the Shasu threatened the "[[Via Maris|Way of Horus]]" north from Gaza. Evidence shows that [[Deir Alla]] ([[Sukkot (place)|Succoth]]) was destroyed, likely by an earthquake, after the reign of the female pharaoh [[Tausret]] (r. 1191–1189&nbsp;BC) though the date of this destruction appears to be much later dating to roughly 1150&nbsp;BC.<ref name=":2">{{Cite journal |last=Millek |first=Jesse |date=2019 |title=Crisis, Destruction, and the End of the Late Bronze Age in Jordan: A Preliminary Survey |journal=Zeitschrift des Deutschen Palästina-Vereins |volume=135 |issue=2 |pages=127–129 |url= https://www.academia.edu/41613136 |url-access=registration |via=Academia.edu |access-date=3 November 2022 |ref={{sfnref|Millek|2019a}} }}</ref>

There is little evidence that any major city or settlement in the Southern Levant was destroyed around 1200&nbsp;BC.<ref name=":3">{{Cite journal |last=Millek |first=Jesse |date=2018 |title=Just how much was destroyed? The end of the Late Bronze Age in the Southern Levant |journal=Ugarit-Forschungen |issue=49 |pages=239–274 |url= https://www.academia.edu/42097042 |url-access=registration |via=Academia.edu |access-date=4 November 2022 |ref={{sfnref|Millek|2018b}} }}</ref> At [[Lachish]], the Fosse Temple III underwent ritual termination, and a house in Area S seems to have burned in a house fire because the most severe burning evidence was located beside two ovens, with no other part of the city exhibiting any burning. After this, the city was rebuilt more lavishly than before.{{sfnp|Millek|2017|pp=127–128}} For Megiddo, most parts of the city did not have any signs of damage and although it is possible that the palace in Area AA might have been destroyed, this is not for certain.<ref name=":3" /> While the monumental structures at Hazor were indeed destroyed, the destruction occurred in the mid-13th century, long before the end of the Late Bronze Age.<ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Ben-Tor |first1=Amnon |last2=Zuckerman |first2=Sharon |date=2008 |title=Hazor at the End of the Late Bronze Age: Back to Basics |journal=Bulletin of the American Schools of Oriental Research |volume=350 |issue=350 |pages=1–6 |doi=10.1086/BASOR25609263 |issn=0003-097X |jstor=25609263 |s2cid=163208536}}</ref> Many sites were not burned to the ground around 1200&nbsp;BC, including Ashkelon, Ashdod, Tell es-Safi, Tel Batash, Tel Burna, Tel Dor, Tel Gerisa, Tell Jemmeh, Khirbet Rabud, Tel Zeror, and Tell Abu Hawam.{{sfnp|Millek|2019b|pp=147–188}}<ref name=":3" /><ref name=":2" /><ref name=":0" />

During the reign of Ramesses III, [[Philistines]] were allowed to resettle the coastal strip from Gaza to Joppa, [[Denyen]] (possibly the [[tribe of Dan]] in the Bible, or more likely the people of Adana, also known as Danuna, part of the Hittite Empire) settled from [[Jaffa|Joppa]] to [[Acre, Israel|Acre]], and [[Tjekker]] in Acre. As the ''[[Story of Wenamun]]'' shows, the sites quickly achieved independence.

Despite many theories claiming that trade relations broke down in the southern Levant after 1200&nbsp;BC, there is ample evidence that trade with other regions continued after the end of the Late Bronze Age. Archaeologist Jesse Millek has shown that while the common assumption is that trade in Cypriot and Mycenaean pottery ended around 1200&nbsp;BC, trade in Cypriot pottery actually largely came to an end in 1300&nbsp;BC, while for Mycenaean pottery, this trade ended in 1250&nbsp;BC; destruction around 1200&nbsp;BC could not have affected either pattern of international trade since both ended before the end of the Late Bronze Age.{{sfnp|Millek|2019b|pp=180–212}}<ref>{{cite book |last=Millek |first=Jesse Michael |chapter=The impact of destruction on trade at the end of the Late Bronze Age in the These sites in the [[Southern Levant]] show evidence of the collapse: [[Akko]], [[Ashdod]], [[Ashkelon]], [[Bethel]], Beth Shemesh, [[Sukkot (place)|Deir 'Alla (Sukkot)]], [[Tel Hazor]], [[Tel Lachish]], and [[Tel Megiddo]].

=== Mesopotamia ===
[[File:Relief of Tiglath-Pileser I.png|thumb|A [[Middle Assyrian Empire|Middle Assyrian]] carved relief depicting [[Tiglath-Pileser I]] ({{reign}}1114–1076&nbsp;BC)]]

The [[Middle Assyrian Empire]] (1392–1056&nbsp;BC) had destroyed the Hurrian-Mitanni Empire, annexed much of the [[Hittite Empire]] and eclipsed the [[Egyptian Empire]]. At the beginning of the Late Bronze Age collapse, it controlled an empire stretching from the [[Caucasus Mountains]] in the north to the [[Arabian Peninsula]] in the south, and from [[ancient Iran]] in the east to [[Cyprus]] in the west. However, in the 12th century&nbsp;BC Assyrian satrapies in [[Anatolia]] came under attack from the [[Mushki]] (who may have been [[Phrygians]]) and those in the Levant from Arameans. [[Tiglath-Pileser I]] (reigned 1114–1076&nbsp;BC) was able to defeat and repel these attacks. The Middle Assyrian Empire survived intact throughout much of this period, with Assyria dominating and often ruling Babylonia directly, and controlling southeastern and southwestern Anatolia, northwestern [[Iran]] and much of northern and central [[Syria]] and [[Canaan]], as far as the [[Mediterranean Sea|Mediterranean]] and Cyprus.<ref name="auto1">Georges Roux, ''Ancient Iraq''</ref>

The Arameans and [[Phrygians]] were subjugated, and Assyria and its colonies were not threatened by the [[Sea Peoples]] who had ravaged Egypt and much of the East Mediterranean. The Assyrians often conquered as far as [[Phoenicia]] and the [[East Mediterranean]]. However, after the death of [[Ashur-bel-kala]] in 1056, Assyria withdrew to areas close to its natural borders, encompassing what is today northern Iraq, northeastern Syria, the fringes of northwestern Iran, and southeastern Turkey. It retained a stable monarchy, the best army in the world, and an efficient civil administration, enabling it to survive the Bronze Age Collapse intact. Assyrian written records remained numerous and the most consistent in the world during the period, and the Assyrians were still able to mount long range military campaigns in all directions when necessary. From the late 10th century&nbsp;BC, Assyria once more asserted itself internationally, and the [[Neo-Assyrian Empire]] grew to be the largest the world had yet seen.<ref name="auto1" />

The situation in Babylonia was very different. After the Assyrian withdrawal, it was still subject to periodic Assyrian (and [[Elamite]]) subjugation. New groups of Semitic speakers such as the [[Aramaeans|Arameans]] and [[Suteans]] (and in the period following the Bronze Age Collapse, [[Chaldea]]ns as well) spread unchecked into Babylonia from the Levant. The weak power of Babylonian kings barely extended beyond the city limits of Babylon. Babylon was sacked by the [[Elam]]ites under [[Shutruk-Nahhunte]] (c. 1185–1155&nbsp;BC) and lost control of the [[Diyala River]] valley to Assyria.

=== Syria ===
Ancient Syria had been initially dominated by a number of indigenous [[Semitic languages|Semitic]]-speaking peoples. The modern term "Syria" is a later Indo-European corruption of "Assyria", which only became formally applied to the Levant during the [[Seleucid Empire]] (323–150&nbsp;BC) (see [[Etymology of Syria]]). The [[East Semitic]]-speaking polities of [[Ebla]] and the [[Akkadian Empire]], the [[Northwest Semitic]]-speaking Amorites ("Amurru"), and the people of Ugarit were prominent among them.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Woodard |first=Roger D. |title=The Ancient Languages of Syria-Palestine and Arabia |date=2008 |publisher=[[Cambridge University Press]] |isbn=978-1-139-46934-0 |page=5 |url= https://books.google.com/books?id=vTrT-bZyuPcC |access-date=29 August 2020}}</ref> Syria during this time was known as "the land of the Amurru".

Before and during the Bronze Age Collapse, specifically between the 15th and late 13th centuries BC, Syria became a battleground for the Hittites, the Middle Assyrian Empire, the Mitanni, and the New Kingdom of Egypt. The Assyrians destroyed the Hurri-Mitanni empire and annexed much of the Hittite empire. The Egyptian empire had withdrawn from the region after failing to overcome the Hittites and, being fearful of the ever-growing Assyrian might, left much of the region under Assyrian control until the late 11th century&nbsp;BC. Later, the coastal regions came under attack from the [[Sea Peoples]]. During this period, from the 12th century&nbsp;BC onward, the incoming [[Northwest Semitic]]–speaking Arameans came to demographic prominence in Syria; the region outside of the Canaanite-speaking [[Phoenicia]]n coastal areas eventually came to speak [[Aramaic]]. This region eventually came to be known as [[Aramea]] and [[Eber Nari]].

The [[Babylonians]] belatedly attempted to gain a foothold in the region during their brief revival under [[Nebuchadnezzar I]] in the 12th century&nbsp;BC but they, too, were overcome by their Assyrian neighbors.

Late Bronze Age Levantine sites previously showed evidence of trade links with [[Mesopotamia]] ([[Sumer]], [[Akkad (region)|Akkad]], Assyria, and [[Babylonia]]), [[Anatolia]] (Hattia, Hurria, Luwia, and later, the Hittites), Egypt, and the [[Aegean Sea#History|Aegean]]. Evidence at Ugarit shows that the destruction occurred after the reign of Merneptah (r. 1213–1203&nbsp;BC) and even the fall of [[Chancellor Bay]] (d. 1192&nbsp;BC). The last Bronze Age king of Ugarit, [[Ammurapi]], was a contemporary of the last-known Hittite king, [[Suppiluliuma II]]. The exact dates of his reign are unknown.

A letter by the king is preserved on one of the clay tablets found baked in the conflagration of the city. Ammurapi stresses the seriousness of the crisis faced by many Levantine states due to attacks. In his response to a plea for assistance from the king of [[Alasiya]], Ammurapi highlights the desperate situation Ugarit faced in letter RS 18.147:<ref>Nougaryol, Jean; et al. (1968) ''Ugaritica'', 5(24): 87–90.</ref>{{full citation needed|date=February 2025|reason=No such Nougaryol source is fully cited anywhere in this article.}}

{{blockquote|My father, behold, the enemy's ships came [here]; my cities[?] were burned, and they did evil things in my country. Does not my father know that all my troops and chariots[?] are in the Land of Hatti, and all my ships are in the [[Lukka lands|Land of Lukka]]? ... Thus, the country is abandoned to itself. May my father know it: the seven ships of the enemy that came here inflicted much damage upon us.}}

Eshuwara, the senior governor of Cyprus, responded in letter RS 20.18:{{sfnp|Cline|2014|p=151}}

{{blockquote|As for the matter concerning those enemies: [It was] the people from your country [and] your own ships [who] did this! And [it was] the people from your country [who] committed these transgression(s) ... I am writing to inform you and protect you. Be aware!}}

The ruler of [[Carchemish]] sent troops to assist Ugarit, but Ugarit had already been sacked. Letter RS 19.011 (KTU 2.61)<ref name="P521115">{{cite journal |last1=Dietrich |first1=Manfried |last2=Loretz |first2=Oswald |last3=Sanmartín |first3=Joaquin |at="Archival view of P521115" sect. |title=The Cuneiform Alphabetic Texts from Ugarit, Ras Ibn Hani and Other Places |journal=Alter Orient und Altes Testament |volume=360 |issue=1 |location=Münster |publisher=Ugarit-Verlag |via=Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative |url= https://cdli.ucla.edu/search/archival_view.php?ObjectID=P521115 |access-date=2 March 2021 |archive-date=9 October 2021 |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20211009150834/https://cdli.ucla.edu/search/archival_view.php?ObjectID=P521115|url-status=dead }}{{bsn|reason=The URL was for a particular illustration. That e-library resource appears to have moved to [https://cdli.mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de/artifacts/521115 a webpage] now hosted by Max Planck Institute for the History of Science, but the actual image does not load there as of February 2025 due to server misconfiguration, and is also not recoverable from the Web.Archive.org copy.|date=March 2026<!--Comment predates this, but was incorrectly included in the footnote text-->}}</ref> sent from Ugarit following the devastation read:<ref name="astour">{{cite journal |last1=Astour |first1=Michael C. |title=New Evidence on the Last Days of Ugarit |journal=American Journal of Archaeology |date=1965 |volume=69 |issue=3 |page=258 |doi=10.2307/502290 |jstor=502290}}</ref>

{{blockquote|To Ž[?]rdn, my lord, say: thy messenger arrived. The degraded one trembles, and the low one is torn to pieces. Our food in the threshing floors is sacked and the vineyards are also destroyed. Our city is sacked, and may you know it!}}

This quote is frequently interpreted as "the degraded one", referring to the army being humiliated, destroyed, or both.{{sfnp|Cline|2014|p=151}} The letter is also quoted with the final statement "Mayst thou know it"/"May you know it" repeated twice for effect in several later sources, while no such repetition appears to occur in the original.

The destruction levels of Ugarit contained Late Helladic IIIB ware, but none dating from LH IIIC (see [[Mycenaean Greece]]). Therefore, the date of the destruction is important for the dating of the LH IIIC phase. Since an Egyptian sword bearing the name of [[Pharaoh]] [[Merneptah]] was found in the destruction levels, 1190&nbsp;BC was selected as the date for the beginning of the LH IIIC. A [[cuneiform]] tablet found in 1986 shows that Ugarit was destroyed after the death of Merneptah. It is generally agreed that Ugarit had already been destroyed by 1178&nbsp;BC, which was the eighth ruling year of [[Ramesses III]]. Letters on clay tablets excavated from the site speak of an attack from the sea, and a letter from [[Alashiya]] ([[Cyprus]]) speaks of cities already having been destroyed by attackers who came by sea.

There is clear evidence that Ugarit was destroyed in some kind of assault, though the exact assailant is not known. In one residential area called the Ville sud, thirty-two arrowheads were found scattered throughout the area. Twelve of these arrowheads were found on the streets and in open spaces. Along with the arrowheads, two lance heads, four javelin heads, five bronze daggers, one bronze sword, and three bronze pieces of armor were scattered throughout the houses and streets, indicating that a fight occurred. An additional twenty-five arrowheads were also found scattered around the city centre. All of the recovered weaponry suggests the city was ruined by an assault, not by an earthquake.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Millek |first=Jesse |date=2020 |title='Our city is sacked. May you know it!' The Destruction of Ugarit and its Environs by the Sea Peoples |journal=Archaeology and History of Lebanon |volume=52–53 |pages=105–108 |url= https://www.academia.edu/44654019 |url-access=registration |via=Academia.edu |access-date=5 November 2022}}</ref> At the city of Emar on the Euphrates, at some time between 1187 and 1175, only the monumental and religious structures were targeted for destruction. The houses, on the other hand, appear to have been emptied, abandoned, and left intact. This particular scenario suggests that Emar was burned by attackers, despite the lack of weapons at the site.{{sfnp|Millek|2019c|pp=167–169}}

While certain cities such as Ugarit and Emar were destroyed at the end of the Late Bronze Age, there are several others which were not destroyed, even though they erroneously appear on most maps of destruction at the end of the Late Bronze Age. No evidence of destruction has been found at Hama, Qatna, Kadesh, Alalakh, and Aleppo, while for Tell Sukas archaeologists only found some minor burning on some floors, likely indicating that the town was not burned to the ground around 1200&nbsp;BC.{{sfnp|Millek|2019c}}

The [[West Semitic languages|West Semitic]] Arameans eventually superseded the earlier Amorites and people of Ugarit. Although the Arameans, [[Phoenicia]]ns, and Syro-Hittite states dominated the region demographically, the Middle Assyrian Empire conquered the Levant and held political and military control until withdrawing in the late 11th century&nbsp;BC, though Assyrian military campaigns in the area persisted. However, with the rise of the [[Neo-Assyrian Empire]] in the late 10th century&nbsp;BC, the entire region once again fell to Assyria.

These sites in Syria show evidence of the collapse: [[Alalakh]], [[Aleppo]], [[Emar]], [[Hama]], [[Kadesh (Syria)|Kadesh]], [[Qatna]], [[Tell Sukas]], and [[Ugarit]].

==Possible causes==

Various mutually compatible explanations for the collapse have been proposed, including climatic changes, migratory invasions by groups such as the [[Sea Peoples]], the spread of iron [[metallurgy]], military developments, and a range of political, social and economic systems failures, but none have achieved consensus. Earthquakes have also been proposed as causal, but recent research suggests that earthquakes were not as influential as previously believed.{{sfnp|Millek|2023}} It is likely that a combination of several factors is responsible.

General systems collapse theory, pioneered by [[Joseph Tainter]], proposes that [[societal collapse]] results from an increase in [[social complexity]] beyond a sustainable level, leading people to revert to simpler ways of life.<ref>Tainter, Joseph (1976). ''The Collapse of Complex Societies'' (Cambridge University Press).</ref> The growing complexity and specialization of the Late Bronze Age political, economic, and social organization made the organization of civilization too intricate to reestablish once seriously disrupted.<ref>Thomas, Carol G.; Conant, Craig. (1999) ''Citadel to City-state: The Transformation of Greece, 1200–700 B.C.E.'',</ref> The critical flaws of the Late Bronze Age (its centralization, specialization, complexity, and top-heavy [[political structure]]) were exposed by sociopolitical events (revolt of peasantry and defection of mercenaries), fragility of all kingdoms (Mycenaean, Hittite, Ugaritic, and Egyptian), demographic crises (overpopulation), and wars between states. Other factors that could have placed increasing pressure on the fragile kingdoms include piracy by the Sea Peoples interrupting maritime trade, as well as drought, crop failure and famine.{{sfnp|Cline|2014}}

=== Drought ===
A diversion of midwinter storms from the Atlantic to north of the [[Pyrenees]] and the Alps brought wetter conditions to Central Europe and drought to the Eastern Mediterranean near the time of the Late Bronze Age collapse.<ref name="middleton" /> During what may have been the driest era of the Late Bronze Age, tree cover of the Mediterranean forest dwindled. Juniper tree ring measurements in the Anatolia region demonstrate a severe dry period from c.&nbsp;1198 to c.&nbsp;1196&nbsp;BC.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Manning |first1=Sturt W. |last2=Kocik |first2=Cindy |last3=Lorentzen |first3=Brita |last4=Sparks |first4=Jed P. |title=Severe multi-year drought coincident with Hittite collapse around 1198–1196 BC |journal=[[Nature (journal)|Nature]] |volume=614 |pages=719–724 |date=February 2023 |issue=7949 |doi=10.1038/s41586-022-05693-y |doi-access=free|pmid=36755095 |pmc=9946833 |bibcode=2023Natur.614..719M }}</ref> In the Dead Sea region (the southern Levant), the subsurface water level dropped by more than {{convert|50|m}} during the end of the second millennium BC. According to the geography of that region, for water levels to drop so drastically the amount of rain the surrounding mountains received would have been dismal.<ref name=":4">{{Cite journal |last1=Knapp |first1=A. Bernard |last2=Manning |first2=Sturt W. |date=2016 |title=Crisis in Context: The End of the Late Bronze Age in the Eastern Mediterranean |journal=American Journal of Archaeology |volume=120 |issue=1 |pages=99–149 |doi=10.3764/aja.120.1.0099 |doi-access=free |s2cid=191385013}}</ref> Using the [[Palmer Drought Index]] across 35 weather stations in Greece, Turkey, and the Middle East, researchers found that a prolonged drought comparable to the one starting in January 1972&nbsp;AD would have impacted every site linked to the Late Bronze Age collapse.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Weiss |first=Harvey |date=June 1982 |title=The decline of Late Bronze Age civilization as a possible response to climatic change |journal=Climatic Change |volume=4 |issue=2 |pages=173–198 |doi=10.1007/BF00140587 |s2cid=154059624}}</ref>

Alternatively, changes at the end of the Bronze Age could be better characterized as a 'gear shift' in Mediterranean climate rather than an event of three years. The long-range shift in precipitation would not have been a crisis event, but rather a continual stress put on societies in the region over several generations. There was no one year where conditions became untenable, "nor one straw that broke the back of the camel".<ref name="Drake influence of climatic change">{{cite journal |last1=Drake |first1=Brandon L. |title=The influence of climatic change on the Late Bronze Age Collapse and the Greek Dark Ages |journal=Journal of Archaeological Science |date=June 2012 |volume=39 |issue=6 |pages=1862–1870 |doi=10.1016/j.jas.2012.01.029 |bibcode=2012JArSc..39.1862D}}</ref>

Analysis of multiple lines of [[Paleoecology|paleoenvironmental]] evidence suggests climate change was one aspect associated with this period, but not the sole cause.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Hazell |first1=Calian J. |last2=Pound |first2=Matthew J. |last3=Hocking |first3=Emma P. |date=23 April 2022 |title=High-resolution Bronze Age palaeoenvironmental change in the eastern Mediterranean: exploring the links between climate and societies |journal=Palynology |volume=46 |issue=4 |article-number=2067259 |bibcode=2022Paly...4667259H |doi=10.1080/01916122.2022.2067259 |s2cid=252971820 |doi-access=free}}</ref> This was also the conclusion reached by Knapp and Manning in 2016 who concluded that, "Based on a series of [[Proxy (climate)|proxy]] indicators, there is clearly some sort of shift to cooler and more arid and unstable conditions generally between the 13th and 10th centuries BC, but not necessarily any one key 'episode'; thus, there is a context for change but not necessarily its only or specific cause."<ref name=":4" /> Moreover, Karakaya and Riehl's recent study of ancient plant remains from Syria showed little evidence that plants underwent water stress during the Late Bronze Age to Iron Age transition. As they summarize their research, "The emerging picture as concerns plant subsistence is that there is no clear evidence that the Late Bronze Age and the Iron Age were periods of dearth and widespread famine, as some climate models have presupposed."<ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Karakaya |first1=Doğa |last2=Riehl |first2=Simone |date=2019–2020 |title=Subsistence in Post-Collapse Societies: Patterns of Agroproduction from the Late Bronze Age to Iron Age in the Northern Levant and Beyond |journal=Archaeology & History in the Lebanon |issue=50–51 |page=155 |url= https://www.academia.edu/43876764 |access-date=31 March 2023}}</ref> A 2012 study suggested a rapid regional drop of 3–4 degrees Celsius in the [[Mediterranean basin|Mediterranean]] and Middle East from the start of the collapse to the end of the [[Greek Dark Ages]] based on measurements of nearby [[sea surface temperatures]] and changes in marine [[plankton]]. Northern Hemisphere temperatures skyrocketed and precipitously plummeted during the collapse (a rapid rise of approximately 0.2 degrees Celsius during the first 10 years, followed by a decrease of 0.2 degrees in the next 40 years), and temperatures continued declining to their lowest levels (3–4 degrees Celsius of cooling) during the Greek Dark Ages before slowly increasing to the [[Roman Warm Period]]. There were rapid changes in [[photosynthetic]] plankton during the collapse. The study provides evidence of rapid precipitation loss during the collapse from the paleoclimate record.<ref name="Drake influence of climatic change" />

=== Ironworking ===
The Bronze Age collapse may be seen in the context of a technological history that saw the slow spread of [[ironworking]] technology from present-day [[Bulgaria]] and [[Romania]] in the 13th and the 12th centuries BC.<ref>{{cite book |last=Stoia |first=Adriana |contribution=The Beginning of Iron Age Technology in Romania |editor1-last=Stig Sørensen |editor1-first=Marie Louise |editor2-last=Thomas |editor2-first=Roger |title=The Bronze Age–Iron Age Transition in Europe |volume=I. D. |title=The Coming of the Age of Iron |publisher=[[Yale University Press]] |date=1980 |isbn=978-0-300-02425-8}}</ref> Leonard R. Palmer suggested that [[Iron (material)|iron]], which is superior to bronze for weapons manufacturing, was in more plentiful supply and so allowed larger armies of iron users to overwhelm the smaller bronze-equipped armies that consisted largely of [[Maryannu]] [[chariotry]].<ref>{{cite book |last=Palmer |first=Leonard R. |date=1962 |title=Mycenaeans and Minoans: Aegean Prehistory in the Light of the Linear B Tablets |location=New York |publisher=[[Alfred A. Knopf]]}}</ref> Patrick R. Fitsimmons uses statistics to hypothesise that access to iron reduced elite power relative to non-elites, leading to increased political fragmentation.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Fitzsimmons |first=Patrick |date=April 22, 2024 |title=Blood and Iron: Political Fragmentation in the Ancient Eastern Mediterranean |url=https://dx.doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4803578 |website=George Mason University}}</ref>

=== Migratory invasions ===
{{Main|Sea Peoples}}

Primary sources report that the era was marked by [[Pre-modern human migration|large-scale migration of people]] at the end of the Late Bronze Age. Drought in the Nile Valley also may have contributed to the rise of the Sea Peoples and their sudden migration across the eastern Mediterranean. It was suspected that crop failures, famine and the population reduction that resulted from the lackluster flow of the Nile and the migration of the Sea Peoples led to New Kingdom Egypt falling into political instability at the end of the Late Bronze Age and well into the Iron Age. A general [[systems collapse]] has been put forward as an explanation for the reversals in culture that occurred between the [[Urnfield culture]] of the 13th and 12th centuries BC and the rise of the Celtic [[Hallstatt culture]] in the 10th and 9th centuries BC.<ref>{{cite web |author=<!--Staff writer(s); no by-line.--> |url= http://www.iol.ie/~edmo/linktoprehistory.html |title=History of Castlemagner |publisher=Castlemagner Historical Society |date=2000 |via=[[Ireland On-Line]] (and [[Internet Archive]]) |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20090416234551/http://www.iol.ie/~edmo/linktoprehistory.html |archive-date=16 April 2009}}</ref>

=== Warfare <span class="anchor" id="Warfare"></span>===
{{See also|Ancient warfare}}

[[Robert Drews]] argues{{sfnp|Drews|1993|pp=192ff}} for the appearance of massed [[infantry]], using newly developed weapons and armour, such as [[Casting (metalworking)|cast]] rather than [[Forging|forged]] spearheads and [[Bronze Age sword#Europe|long swords]], a revolutionizing cut-and-thrust weapon,{{sfnp|Drews|1993|p=194}} and [[javelin]]s. The appearance of bronze foundries suggests "that mass production of bronze artefacts was suddenly important in the Aegean". For example, [[Homer]] uses "spears" as a [[metonymy|metonym]] for "warriors".

Such new weaponry, in the hands of large numbers of "running [[skirmisher]]s", who could swarm and cut down a chariot army, would destabilize states that were based upon the use of chariots by the ruling class. That would precipitate an abrupt social collapse as raiders began to conquer, loot and burn cities.{{sfnp|Drews|1993}}<ref>{{cite web |last=McGoodwin |first=Michael |title=Drews (Robert) End of Bronze Age Summary |website=McGoodwin.net |url= https://mcgoodwin.net/pages/otherbooks/rd_endbrozeage.html |access-date=2 January 2007 |url-status=live |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20111227021238/http://www.mcgoodwin.net/pages/otherbooks/rd_endbrozeage.html |archive-date=27 December 2011}}</ref>

==Aftermath==
Robert Drews described the collapse as "arguably the worst disaster in ancient history, even more calamitous than the [[collapse of the Western Roman Empire]]".<ref>Drews 1993: 3</ref> Cultural memories of the disaster told of a "lost [[golden age]]". For example, [[Hesiod]] spoke of Ages of Gold, Silver, and Bronze, separated from the cruel modern Age of Iron by the [[Greek Heroic Age|Age of Heroes]]. Rodney Castleden suggests that memories of the Bronze Age collapse influenced [[Plato]]'s story of [[Atlantis]] in ''[[Timaeus (dialogue)|Timaeus]]'' and the ''[[Critias (dialogue)|Critias]]''.

Only a few powerful states survived the Bronze Age collapse, particularly [[Assyria]] (albeit temporarily weakened), the New Kingdom of Egypt (also weakened), the [[Phoenicia]]n city-states and [[Elam]]. Even among these comparative survivors, success was mixed. By the end of the 12th century BC, Elam waned after its defeat by [[Nebuchadnezzar I]], who briefly revived Babylonian fortunes before suffering a series of defeats by the Assyrians. After the death of [[Ashur-bel-kala]] in 1056&nbsp;BC, Assyria declined for a century. Its empire shrank significantly by 1020&nbsp;BC, apparently leaving it in control only of the areas in its immediate vicinity, although its heartland remained well-defended. By the time of [[Wenamun]], Phoenicia had regained independence from Egypt.

Gradually, by the end of the ensuing Dark Age, remnants of the Hittites coalesced into small [[Neo-Hittite states|Syro-Hittite states]] in [[Cilicia]] and in the Levant, where the new states were composed of mixed Hittite and [[Arameans|Aramean]] polities. Beginning in the mid-10th century BC, a series of small Aramean kingdoms formed in the Levant, and the [[Philistines]] settled in southern Canaan, where Canaanite speakers had coalesced into a number of polities such as [[Kingdom of Israel (united monarchy)|Israel]], [[Moab]], [[Edom]] and [[Ammon]].
