{{Short description|Human demography in Europe and the Mediterranean during the Middle Ages}}
[[File:Les Très Riches Heures du duc de Berry octobre detail.jpg|thumb|right|270px|Peasants preparing the fields next to the medieval [[Louvre Castle]] for the winter with a [[harrow (tool)|harrow]] and sowing for the winter grain, from ''[[The Very Rich Hours of the Duke of Berry]]'', {{Circa|1410}}]]

'''Medieval demography''' is the study of human [[demography]] in [[Europe]] and the Mediterranean during the [[Middle Ages]]. It estimates and seeks to explain the number of people who were alive during the Medieval period, population trends, life expectancy, family structure, and related issues. Demography is considered a crucial element of [[History|historical]] change throughout the Middle Ages.

The population of Europe remained at a low level in the Early Middle Ages, boomed during the High Middle Ages and reached a peak around 1300, then a number of calamities caused a steep decline, the nature of which historians have debated. Population levels began to recover around the late 15th century, gaining momentum in the early 16th century.

The science of medieval demography relies on various lines of evidence, such as administrative records, wills and other types of records, archaeological field data, economic data, and written histories. Because the data are often incomplete and/or ambiguous, there can be significant disagreement among medieval demographers.

==Demographic history of Europe==
The population levels of Europe during the Middle Ages can be roughly categorized:<ref name=numbers/>

*400–600 (Late Antiquity): population decline
*600–1000 (Early Middle Ages): stable at a low level, with intermittent growth.
*1000–1250 (High Middle Ages): population boom and expansion.
*1250–1348 (Late Middle Ages): stable or intermittently rising at a high level, [[Great Famine of 1315–1317|with fall in 1315–17]] in most of Europe.
*1348–1420 (Late Middle Ages): steep decline in England and France, growth in East Central Europe.
*1420–1470 (Late Middle Ages): stable or intermittently falling to a low level in Western Europe, growth in East Central Europe.
*1470–onward: slow expansion gaining momentum in the early 16th century.

===Late Antiquity===
[[Late Antiquity]] saw various indicators of Roman civilization beginning to decline, including urbanization, seaborne commerce, and total population. Only 40% as many Mediterranean shipwrecks have been found for the 3rd century as for the 1st.<ref>{{cite journal |first= Keith  |last= Hopkins |title= Taxes and Trade in the Roman Empire (200 B.C.–A.D. 400) |journal= Journal of Roman Studies |volume= 70 |pages= 101–25 |year= 1980 |doi= 10.2307/299558 |jstor= 299558 |s2cid= 162507113 }}</ref> During the period from 150 to 400, with the intermittent appearance of plague, the [[demographics of the Roman Empire|population of the Roman Empire]] ranged from a high of 70 to a low of 50 million, followed by a fairly good recovery if not to the previous highs of the Early Empire. Serious gradual depopulation began in the West only in the 5th century, and in the East due to the appearance of bubonic plague in 541. This followed 250 years of economic growth, which began after the troubles which afflicted the empire from the 250s to 270s.  Proximate causes of the population decrease include the [[Antonine Plague]] (165–180), the [[Plague of Cyprian]] (250 to {{circa}} 260), and the [[Crisis of the Third Century]]. European population probably reached a minimum during the [[extreme weather events of 535–536]] and the ensuing [[Plague of Justinian]] (541–542). Some have connected this demographic transition to the [[:File:2000 Year Temperature Comparison.png|Migration Period Pessimum]],{{Clarify|date= December 2012}} when a decrease in global temperatures impaired agricultural yields.<ref>

===Early Middle Ages===
{{See also|Burghal Hidage}}

A major [[Plague of Justinian|plague epidemic]] struck the Mediterranean, and much of Europe, in the 6th century.

The [[Early Middle Ages]] saw relatively little population growth with urbanization well below its Roman peak, reflecting a low technological level, limited trade and political, social and economic dislocation exacerbated by the impact of [[Viking expansion]] in the north, [[Early Muslim conquests|Arab expansion]] in the south and the movement of [[Slav]]s and [[Bulgars|Bulgarians]], and later the [[Hungarian people|Magyars]] in the east.<ref name=numbers/> This rural, uncertain life spurred the development of [[feudalism]] and the [[Christianization]] of Europe.<ref name=numbers/> Estimates of the total population of Europe are speculative, but at the time of [[Charlemagne]] it is thought to have been between 25 and 30 million, of which perhaps half were in the [[Carolingian]] Empire that covered modern France, the Low Countries, western Germany, Austria, Slovenia, northern Italy and part of northern Spain.<ref name=numbers/> Most medieval settlements remained small, with agricultural land and large zones of unpopulated and lawless [[wilderness]] in between (Geographers estimate that around 800, as much as three-quarters of Europe was still forested).<ref name=numbers/>
Population density was only two to five persons per square kilometer in Britain, similarly in Germany, and somewhat higher in France.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Chapelot |first1=Jean |last2=Fossier |first2=Robert |title=The village [and] house in the Middle Ages |date=1985 |publisher=Univ. of California Pr |location=Berkeley, Calif. u.a |isbn=0-520-04669-2 |pages=15 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=c3J4VKlyOsMC&q=Population+density+was+only+two+to+five}}</ref>

Manorial surveys and some allusions to provincial hearth taxes suggest a population of 5 million for Carolingian France. Presumed densities of settlement support estimates of 4 million for Italy and a similar number for Iberia, as well as German lands (including Scandinavia); 6 million for Slavic lands and perhaps 2 million for Greece and southern Balkans; 1.5 million people for the entire British Isles.<ref name="New York : Charles Scribner's Sons">{{cite book |author1=David Herlihy |title=Dictionary of the Middle Ages, vol. IV |date=1982 |publisher=New York : Charles Scribner's Sons |isbn=978-0-684-19073-0 |pages=136–141 |url=https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofmidd0004unse_c6a6/mode/2up |chapter=Medieval Demography}}</ref>

===High Middle Ages===
{{See also|Domesday Book}}
[[File:Bevölkerung Mitteleuropas um 895.jpg|thumb|upright=1.3|German [[Ostsiedlung|eastward expansion]], 895—1400]]
In the 10th–13th centuries, agriculture expanded into the wilderness, in what has been termed the "great clearances".<ref name=bartlett/> During the [[High Middle Ages]], many forests and marshes were cleared and cultivated.<ref name=bartlett/> At the same time, during the [[Ostsiedlung]], Germans resettled east of the [[Elbe]] and [[Saale]] rivers, in regions previously only sparsely populated by [[Polabian Slavs]].<ref name=bartlett/> Crusaders expanded to the [[Crusader states]], parts of the [[Iberian Peninsula]] were [[Reconquista|reconquered from the Moors]], and the [[Normans]] colonized England and [[southern Italy]].<ref name=bartlett/> These movements and conquests are part of a larger pattern of population expansion and resettlement that occurred in Europe at this time.<ref name=bartlett>{{cite book |first=Robert |last=Bartlett |title=The Making of Europe: Conquest, Colonization, and Cultural Change, 950–1350 |publisher=Princeton University Press |year=1994 |isbn=0-691-03780-9 |url=https://archive.org/details/makingofeuropeco0000bart|url-access=registration }}</ref>

Reasons for this expansion and colonization include an improving climate known as the [[Medieval warm period]], which resulted in longer and more productive growing seasons; the end of the raids by [[Vikings]], [[Early Muslim conquests|Arabs]], and [[Hungarian invasions of Europe|Magyars]], resulting in greater political stability; advancements in [[medieval technology]] allowing more land to be farmed; 11th-century reforms of the Church that further increased social stability; and the rise of [[Feudalism]], which also brought a measure of social stability.<ref name=numbers/> Towns and trade revived, and the rise of a money economy began to weaken the bonds of [[serfdom]] that tied peasants to the land.<ref name=numbers/> Land was at first plentiful while labour to clear and work the land was scarce; lords who owned the land found new ways to attract and keep labour.<ref name=numbers/> Urban centres were able to attract serfs with the promise of freedom.<ref name=numbers/> As new regions were settled, both internally and externally, population naturally increased.<ref name=numbers/>

Overall, European population tripled between the years 1000 to 1348<ref>By 1300, the population of western Europe (that is, west of Slavic and Balkan territory) was about 54 millions. {{cite journal |last1=Russell |first1=Josiah Cox |title=Population During the Renaissance |journal=Explorations in Renaissance Culture |date=2 December 1976 |volume=3 |pages=21–22 |doi=10.1163/23526963-90000017 |pmid=12338861 |url=https://brill.com/view/journals/erc/3/1/article-p21_3.xml |access-date=28 November 2023 |language=en|url-access=subscription }}</ref> and is estimated to have reached a peak of 73.5 million to as high as 100 million,<ref name="numbers">The citation combines sources from {{harvnb|Herlihy|1982}}, and from {{cite book |last=Russell |first=Josiah C. |title=The Middle Ages |publisher=Collins/Fontana |year=1972 |editor-last=Cipolla |editor-first=Carlo M. |series=The Fontana Economic History of Europe |volume=1 |pages=[https://archive.org/details/fontanaeconomich0000unse_z6m6/page/24/mode/2up?q=%22population+in+europe%22 25–71] |chapter=Population in Europe |chapter-url=https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/source/pop-in-eur.asp}}</ref> which is substantially higher than the population of the Roman Empire at its peak.<ref name="Seidel, Walter 2007">{{cite book |first=Walter |last=Seidel |title=The Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World |publisher=Cambridge University Press |year=2007 |isbn=9780521780537}}</ref>

* '''England''' – The population of England, between 1.25 and 2 million in 1086,<ref>However, these figures are much lower than the 4 million people there are estimated to have been in Roman times.{{cite web |title=Life in the 11th Century: Population |work=The Domesday Book Online |url=http://www.domesdaybook.co.uk/life.html#6}}</ref> is estimated to have grown to somewhere between 3.7 million<ref name="Russell, Josiah Cox 1972. p. 122">{{cite book |first=Josiah Cox |last=Russell |title=Medieval regions and their cities |publisher=Indiana University Press |year=1972 |isbn=0253337356 |series=Studies in historical geography |page=122}}</ref> and 5–7 million,<ref name=numbers/> although the 14th-century estimates derive from sources after the first plague epidemics, and the estimates for pre-plague population depends on assumed plague mortality, the proportion of children and the rate of omissions in returns of taxable population.<ref name="Russell, Josiah Cox 1972. p. 122"/>

* '''Italy''' – Italy's population around 1300 has been variously estimated at between 10 and 13 million. The two largest cities in Italy, Venice and Florence, had about 100,000 persons each. The larger cities constituted as high as twenty percent of Italy's population.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Russell |first1=Josiah Cox |title=Population During the Renaissance |journal=Explorations in Renaissance Culture |date=2 December 1976 |volume=3 |pages=21–22 |doi=10.1163/23526963-90000017 |pmid=12338861 |url=https://brill.com/view/journals/erc/3/1/article-p21_3.xml |access-date=28 November 2023 |language=en|url-access=subscription }}</ref> By 1300, the population of the entire province of Tuscany may have then surpassed 2 million people — a level the region would not reach again until after 1850.<ref name="New York : Charles Scribner's Sons"/>

* '''France''' – In 1328, France is believed to have supported between 15 and 17 million people (in a smaller geographical area than today's) and 20 million people (in the present-day area),<ref>{{harvnb|Herlihy|1982}}</ref><ref>{{Harvnb|Duby|1977}}</ref> the latter not reached again until the [[early modern period]].<ref>{{Cite web |last=Klein Goldewijk |first=Kees |date=30 November 2023 |title=Population by region since 10,000 BCE |url=https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/population?time=700..latest&country=~FRA |access-date=1 July 2025 |website=Our World in Data |publisher=Global Change Data Lab |quote=Based on data from the HYDE database |ref=OWID - HYDE}}</ref>
* '''Kingdom of Hungary''' – The population of the Carpathian Basin probably did not exceed 1 million at the beginning of the 12th century and it may have been between one and two million before the Mongol (Tatar) invasion of 1240. The extent of destruction is reflected in low population growth in the subsequent period. Even in the early 14th century the population was only slightly higher, between 1.4 and 2.3 million.<ref>It is estimated that population density in Carpathian Basin rose from 3.6 persons per square kilometer in 1100, to 10.3 persons/km2 by 1440.{{cite book |last1=Editorial board |title=National atlas of Hungary |date=2018 |publisher=Hungarian academy of sciences, Research centre for astronomy and earth sciences |location=Budapest |isbn=978-963-9545-64-9 |pages=16 |url=https://www.nemzetiatlasz.hu/MNA/National-Atlas-of-Hungary_Vol3_Ch2.pdf}}</ref> In the fourteenth century, under Angevin dynasty (1308-1386), the population of the Kingdom reached around 3 million, before the Plague.<ref>Miklós Molnár, [https://books.google.com/books?id=y0g4YEp7ZrsC&dq=Hungary+medieval+population+million&pg=PA42 A concise history of Hungary], Cambridge University Press, 2001, p. 42</ref> Transylvania, in the eastern part of the Kingdom, had around 550.000 people by 1300.<ref name="Istoria Romaniei">{{cite book |last1=Bărbulescu |first1=Mihai |last2=Deletant |first2=Dennis |author-link2=Dennis Deletant |last3=Hitchins |first3=Keith |author-link3=Keith Hitchins |last4=Papacostea |first4=Șerban |author-link4=Șerban Papacostea |first5=Pompiliu |last5=Teodor |title=Istoria Romaniei |date=1998 |publisher=Editura Enciclopedica |location=Bucuresti |pages=165 |url=https://www.academia.edu/33247269}}</ref>
* '''Wallachia''' – The region in the Southern part of modern Romania had a population of around 400,000 in the fifteenth century.<ref name="Istoria Romaniei"/>
* '''Bulgaria''' – The population of the territories forming modern Bulgaria grew from around 1.1 million in the year 700 to 2.6 million in 1365.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Arkadiev |first1=D. |title=[The population of Bulgaria during the Middle Ages (seventh to fourteenth centuries)] |journal=Naselenie (Sofia, Bulgaria: 1983) |date=1986 |volume=4 |issue=2 |pages=3–11 |pmid=12280532 |issn=0205-0617}}</ref>
* '''Constantinople''' – In 1203 the population of Constantinople stood 400,000<ref name="History of Istanbul">{{cite web |title=History of Istanbul |url=https://istanbultarihi.ist/460-the-population-of-constantinople-in-the-byzantine-period |website=istanbultarihi.ist |access-date=28 November 2023}}</ref> to 500,000; when the Byzantines reclaimed the city in 1261 there were only about 35,000 inhabitants left.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Madden |first1=Thomas |title=Crusades: The Illustrated History |date=1995 |publisher=University of Michigan Press |pages=113 |url=https://archive.org/details/MaddenTFCrusadesTheIllustratedHistory/page/n31/mode/2up |language=English}}</ref> The population of the city stood between 40,000 and 50,000 by the 1450s.<ref>{{cite web |title=Fall of Constantinople {{!}} Facts, Summary, & Significance {{!}} Britannica |url=https://www.britannica.com/event/Fall-of-Constantinople-1453 |website=www.britannica.com |language=en}}</ref> The number of people captured by the Ottomans after the fall of the city was around 33,000.<ref name="History of Istanbul"/>
* '''Kievan Rus''' – the [[Demographic history of Russia|population of Kievan Rus]] is estimated to be between 4.5 million and 8 million, in the absence of historical sources these estimates are based on the assumed population density.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Gorskaya |first1=Natalia |title=Историческая демография России эпохи феодализма: итоги и проблемы изучения|date=1994 |publisher=Наука |isbn=9785020097506 |language=ru|pages=48–49}}</ref>

===Late Middle Ages===
[[File:Doutielt3.jpg|thumb|upright=1.3|Citizens of [[Tournai]] bury plague victims.]]
By the 14th century, the frontiers of settled cultivation had ceased to expand and internal colonization was coming to an end, but population levels remained high. Then a series of events—sometimes called the [[Crisis of the Late Middle Ages]]—collectively killed millions. Starting with the [[Great Famine of 1315–1317|Great Famine]] in 1315 and the [[Black Death]] from 1348, the population of Europe fell abruptly. The period between 1348 and 1420 saw the heaviest loss. In parts of Germany, about 40% of the named inhabitants disappeared.<ref name=numbers/> The population of [[Provence]] was reportedly halved and in some parts of [[Tuscany]], 70% were lost during this period.<ref name=numbers/>

Historians have struggled to explain why so many died.<ref name=numbers/> Some have questioned the long-standing theory that the decline in population was caused only by infectious disease (see further discussions at [[Black Death]]) and so historians have examined other social factors, as follows.

A classic [[Malthusian]] argument has been put forward that Europe was [[Human overpopulation|overpopulated]]: even in good times it was barely able to feed its population.<ref name=numbers/> Grain yields in the 14th century were between 2:1 and 7:1 (2:1 means for every seed planted, 2 are harvested.<ref name=numbers/> Modern grain yields are 30:1 or more.)<ref name=numbers/> Malnutrition developed gradually over decades, lowering resistance to disease, and competition for resources meant more warfare, and then finally crop yields were pushed down by the [[Little Ice Age]].<ref name=numbers/>

An alternative theory is that competition for resources exacerbated the imbalance between property-owners and workers,<ref name=numbers/> and that the money supply ceased to keep up with fixed increased economic activity (being [[commodity money]] based principally on silver)<ref name=numbers/> so that wages sank while rents rose,<ref name=numbers/> leading to demographic stagnation. The economic conditions of the poor also aggravated the calamities of the plague because they had no recourse, such as fleeing to a villa in the country in the manner of the nobles in the ''[[Decameron]]''.<ref name=numbers/> The poor lived in crowded conditions and could not isolate the sick, and had weaker immunities from a deficient diet, difficult living and working conditions and poor sanitation.<ref name=numbers/> After the plague and other exogenous causes of population decline lowered the labor supply, wages increased.<ref name=numbers/> This increased the mobility of labour and led to a [[redistribution of wealth]], although property-owners' attempts to resist change through wage freezes and price controls<ref name=numbers/> contributed to [[Popular revolt in late medieval Europe|popular uprisings]] such as the [[Peasants' Revolt]] of 1381. By 1450, the total population of Europe was substantially below that of 150 years earlier, but all classes overall had a higher standard of living.<ref name=numbers/>

===The Brenner Debate===

Another theory, as introduced by [[Robert Brenner|Robert Paul Brenner]] in a 1976 paper, is that the economic system of the High Middle Ages limited population growth.<ref name="Brenner">{{cite journal |last1=Brenner |first1=Robert |title=Agrarian Class Structure and Economic Development in Pre-Industrial Europe |journal=Past & Present |date=1976 |issue=70 |pages=30–75 |doi=10.1093/past/70.1.30 |jstor=650345 }}</ref><ref>{{Cite book|url=https://searchworks.stanford.edu/view/1599074|title=The Brenner debate: Agrarian class structure and economic development in pre-industrial Europe|series=Past and present publications|year=1985|publisher=Cambridge University Press}}</ref> [[Overlord|Feudal lords]] and [[landlord]]s controlled most of [[Europe]]'s land; they could charge high enough rents or demand a large enough percentage of [[peasant]]s' [[Profit margin|profit]] that peasants on these lands were forced to survive at [[Subsistence economy|subsistence levels]]. With any surplus of [[food]], labor, and [[income]] absorbed by the landowners, the peasants did not have enough [[Capital (economics)|capital]] to [[Investment (economics)|invest]] in their [[farm]]s or enough [[incentive]] to increase the [[productivity]] of their land.

In addition, the small size of most peasants' farms inhibited centralized and more efficient cultivation of land on larger fields. In regions of Europe where [[primogeniture]] was less widely practiced, peasant lands were subdivided and re-subdivided with each generation of [[Inheritance|heirs]]; Brenner writes that consequently: "This too naturally reduced the general level of peasant income, the surplus available for potential investment in agriculture, and the slim hope of agricultural innovation."<ref name="Brenner" />

As a result, according to Brenner, on account of the social and economic system, the size of Europe's population was limited; the [[Agriculture in the Middle Ages|existing agricultural system]] and [[medieval technology]] could not support a population beyond a certain size. When the population of Europe surpassed the threshold that the existing economic structure permitted: population loss, social instability, and famine could result. Only through modifying the existing social structure of land ownership and distribution could Europe's population surpass early 14th century levels.<ref name="Brenner" />

Regardless of the cause, populations continued to fall into the 15th century and remained low into the 16th because the plague returned in cycles over the course of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, although subsequent plagues, such as the "children's plagues" of the 1360s were less virulent than the Great Plague of 1347–1348.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Cohn JR |first1=Samuel K |title=4 Epidemiology of the Black Death and Successive Waves of Plague |journal=Medical History. Supplement |date=2008 |volume=52 |issue=27 |pages=74–100 |doi=10.1017/S0025727300072100 |pmid=18575083 |pmc=2630035 }}</ref>

==Science and art of medieval demography==
Sources traditionally used by modern demographers, such as marriage, birth and death records, are often not available for this period, so scholars rely on other sources, such as archaeological surveys, and written records when available.<ref name=numbers/>

Examples of field data include the physical size of a settlement, and how it grows over time, and the [[Abandoned village|appearance, or disappearance, of settlements]].<ref name=numbers/> For example, after the [[Black Death]] the archaeological record shows the abandonment of upwards of 25% of all villages in [[Spain]].<ref name=numbers/> However, archaeological data are often difficult to interpret.<ref name=numbers/> It is often difficult to assign a precise age to discoveries. Also, some of the largest and most important sites are still occupied and cannot be investigated.<ref name=numbers/> Available archaeological records may be concentrated on the more peripheral regions, for example early Middle Ages [[Anglo-Saxons|Anglo–Saxon]] burials at [[Sutton Hoo]], in East Anglia in England, for which otherwise no records exist.<ref name=numbers/>

Because of these limitations, much of our knowledge comes from written records: descriptive and administrative accounts. Descriptive accounts include those of chroniclers who wrote about the size of armies, victims of [[List of wars|war]] or [[famine]], participants in an oath. However these cannot be relied on as accurate, and are most useful as supporting evidence rather than being taken factually on their own.

The most important written accounts are those contained in administrative records.<ref name=numbers/> These accounts are more objective and accurate because the motivation for writing them was not to influence others.<ref name=numbers/> These records can be divided into two categories: surveys and serial documents. Surveys cover an estate or region on a particular date, rather like a modern inventory.<ref name=numbers/> [[Manorial]] surveys were very common throughout the Middle Ages, in particular in France and England, but faded as serfdom gave way to a money economy.<ref name=numbers/> Fiscal surveys came with the rise of the money economy, the most famous and earliest being the [[Domesday Book]] in 1086.<ref name=numbers/> The Book of Hearths from Italy in 1244 is another example. The largest fiscal survey was of France in 1328. As kings continued to look for new ways to raise money, these fiscal surveys increased in number and scope over time. Surveys have limitations, because they are only a snapshot in time; they do not show long-term trends, and they tend to exclude elements of society.<ref name=numbers/>

Serial records come in different forms.<ref name=numbers/> The earliest are from the 8th century and are land conveyances, such as sales, exchanges, donations, and leases.<ref name=numbers/> Other types of serial records include death records from religious institutions and baptismal registrations. Other helpful records include [[heriot]]s, court records, [[food prices]] and rent prices, from which inferences can be made.<ref name=numbers/>

==Major scholars on medieval demography==
*[[Thomas Robert Malthus]] – founder of demography centered the Malthusian model of economic history.
*[[Michael Postan]] – prominent scholar of the Malthusian model of medieval demographics.
*[[Robert Brenner]] – prominent scholar of the Marxist model of medieval demographics, centered on social class and economic structure instead of population growth alone.

*[[Fernand Braudel]]
