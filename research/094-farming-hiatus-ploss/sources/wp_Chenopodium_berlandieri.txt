'''''Chenopodium berlandieri''''', also known by the common names '''pitseed goosefoot''',<ref name=BSBI07>{{BSBI 2007 |access-date=2014-10-17 }}</ref>  '''lamb's quarters''' (or '''lambsquarters'''), and '''''huauzontle''''' ([[Nahuatl]]), is an [[annual plant|annual]] [[herbaceous plant]] in the family [[Amaranthaceae]].

The species is widespread in [[North America]], where its [[Range (biology)|range]] extends from [[Canada]] south to [[Michoacán]], [[Mexico]]. It is found in every [[U.S. state]] except [[Hawaii]].<ref name=":4" /> The fast-growing, upright plant can reach heights of more than 3 m. It can be differentiated from most of the other members of its large genus by its honeycomb-pitted seeds, and further separated by its serrated, evenly lobed (more or less) lower leaves.<ref name=flora>{{citation |last1=Clemants |first1=Steven E. |last2=Mosyakin |first2=Sergei L. |contribution=Chenopodium berlandieri |year=2004 |title=Flora of North America |volume=4 |page=294 |contribution-url=http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=1&taxon_id=242415441 |place=New York & Oxford |publisher=Oxford University Press}}</ref>

Although widely regarded as a [[weed]], this species was once one of several plants cultivated by [[Native Americans in the United States|Native Americans]] in [[prehistoric]] North America as part of the [[Eastern Agricultural Complex]]. ''C. berlandieri'' was a [[domestication|domesticated]] [[pseudocereal]] crop, similar to the closely related [[quinoa]] ''C. quinoa.''<ref name=":0">{{cite journal|last1=Jarvis|first1=David E.|last2=Ho|first2=Yung Shwen|last3=Lightfoot|first3=Damien J.|last4=Schmöckel|first4=Sandra M.|last5=Li|first5=Bo|last6=Borm|first6=Theo J. A.|last7=Ohyanagi|first7=Hajime|last8=Mineta|first8=Katsuhiko|last9=Michell|first9=Craig T.|date=February 2017|title=The genome of Chenopodium quinoa|journal=Nature|volume=542|issue=7641|pages=307–312|doi=10.1038/nature21370|pmid=28178233|issn=1476-4687|bibcode=2017Natur.542..307J|doi-access=free|hdl=10754/622874|hdl-access=free}}</ref><ref>{{Cite book|title=Social Perspectives on Ancient Lives from Paleoethnobotanical Data|last1=Fritz|first1=Gayle J.|last2=Bruno|first2=Maria C.|last3=Langlie|first3=BrieAnna S.|last4=Smith|first4=Bruce D.|last5=Kistler|first5=Logan|chapter=Cultigen Chenopods in the Americas: A Hemispherical Perspective |date=2017|publisher=Springer, Cham|isbn=978-3-319-52847-2|pages=55–75|doi=10.1007/978-3-319-52849-6_3}}</ref> It continues to be cultivated in Mexico as a [[pseudocereal]], as a [[leaf vegetable]], and for its [[broccoli]]-like [[Panicle|flowering shoots]].

== Description ==
[[File:Chenopodium berlandieri (3767482897).jpg|thumb|upright|The leaf of ''C. berlandieri'']]

=== Leaves and stems ===
The [[Leaf|leaves]] are alternate, {{convert|1/2|to|6|in|order=flip}} long and up to {{convert|3+1/2|in|order=flip}} wide. The leaves are variable in shape: diamond to triangular to egg-shaped to lance-elliptic in outline. The tips may be pointed or blunt, while wedge-shaped or straight across at the base tapering to a stalk up to {{convert|3+1/2|in|order=flip}} long. The lower leaves are largest, irregularly toothed, {{frac|1|1|2}} to 2 or more times as long as wide and usually with a pair of shallow lobes near the base. Leaves become smaller and less toothy as they ascend the stem with the uppermost leaves often much narrower, proportionately longer and toothless.<ref name=":5b">{{Cite web |title=Chenopodium berlandieri (Pitseed Goosefoot): Minnesota Wildflowers |url=https://www.minnesotawildflowers.info/flower/pitseed-goosefoot |access-date=2021-11-18 |website=www.minnesotawildflowers.info |language=en}}</ref>

Surfaces are green, hairless and moderately to densely white-mealy, especially when young. The upper surface usually becomes smooth, while the lower surface usually remains white-mealy. Stems are also highly variable: erect to ascending, unbranched to much branched and sparsely to densely white-mealy, especially on the upper stem. The stem color may vary from green to purple-striped to red.<ref name=":5b" />

=== Flowers ===
Very small flowers are tightly packed in small round clusters (glomerules) in spike-like and branching arrangements at the top of the stem, at the tips of branching stems and arising from upper leaf axils. The glomerules usually crowd on the branch. Within a glomerule, flowers may be at different stages of development: some just budding and others with maturing fruit.<ref name=":5b" /> Flowers lack petals, have 5 [[stamen]]s and a round, green [[Ovary (botany)|ovary]] with a 2-parted style at the tip that is not divided all the way to the base. Cupping the flower is a green calyx with 5 lobes 0.5 to 1.5&nbsp;mm long and variable shape: triangular or egg-shaped, strongly keeled, blunt to rounded at the tip and thin and papery around the edges. Bracts are leaf-like or sometimes absent. The calyx, stalks and branches are moderately to densely white-mealy.<ref name=":5b" />

=== Seeds ===
[[File:Chenopodium berlandieri seeds.jpg|thumb|''Chenopodium berlandieri'' seeds]]
''Chenopodium'' seeds vary in shape between lenticular and [[cylindrical]].<ref name=":3" /> The lenticular shape is more typical of wild members of the species while cylindrical seeds (said to have a "truncated margin") predominate in domesticated varieties.<ref name=":3" /><ref name=":6" />

The nutritive [[perisperm]] tissue is encircled by the [[Hypocotyl|embryo]] along the seed margin. The [[radicle]] protrudes slightly, producing a visible bump in the [[circumference]] of the seed (called the "beak").<ref name=":7">{{cite journal|last=Gremillion|first=Kristen J.|date=1993|title=The evolution of seed morphology in domesticated Chenopodium: an archaeological case study|journal=Journal of Ethnobiology|volume=13|pages=149–169}}</ref> Surrounding the perisperm and embryo are three layers: the [[Seed-coat|inner epiderm]], the [[Seed-coat|outer epiderm]], and the [[pericarp]].  The inner epiderm is also called a [[Seed-coat|tegmen]]. The outer epiderm is synonymous with [[Testa (botany)|testa]]. Together, the outer and inner epiderm make up the [[Seed-coat|seed coat]]. In ''Chenopodium'' literature, the terms outer epiderm, testa, and seed coat are often used interchangeably.<ref name=":3" />

The pericarp is often [[dehiscent]], but is non-dehiscent in some varieties.<ref name=":8">{{cite journal|last1=Asch|first1=David L|last2=Asch|first2=Nancy B|date=1977|title=Chenopod as cultigen: A re-evaluation of some prehistoric collections from eastern North America|journal=Midcontinental Journal of Archaeology|pages=3–45}}</ref> In domesticated varieties, the seed coat may be reduced or absent.<ref name=":3" /> Uniform seed assemblages with seed coats less than 20&nbsp;μm thick are considered to represent domesticated population.<ref name=":6" /><ref name=":7" /><ref name=":3" /> Conversely, wild populations tend to produce seeds with seed coat thicker than 20&nbsp;μm.<ref name=":6" /><ref name=":7" />

==Taxonomy==
The species includes two [[subspecies]]: the [[biological type|type]] subspecies (i.e. ''C. b.'' ssp. ''berlandieri'') and ''C. b.'' ssp. ''nuttalliae''.<ref name=smith2006>{{cite journal| last =Smith| first =Bruce D.| year =2006| title =Eastern North America as an Independent Center of Plant Domestication| journal=Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America| pages =12223–12228| volume=103| number=33| pmid =16894156| doi =10.1073/pnas.0604335103| pmc =1567861| bibcode =2006PNAS..10312223S| doi-access =free}}</ref> The latter, which also goes by the common names '''''[[huauzontle]]''''', '''''huauthili'''''  and '''Nuttall's goosefoot''',<ref>{{GRIN | name = ''Chenopodium berlandieri'' Moq.| id = 104843| access-date = 2009-01-03}}</ref> is a domesticated variety cultivated in Mexico. 
    
As many as six [[extant taxon|extant]] [[variety (biology)|varieties]] of ''C. b.'' ssp. ''berlandieri'' have been identified:<ref name=flora/>
*''C. b.'' subsp. ''berlandieri ''var. ''berlandieri''
*''C. b.'' subsp. ''berlandieri'' var. ''boscianum''
*''C. b.'' subsp. ''berlandieri'' var. ''bushianum'' (Bush's goosefoot)
*''C. b.'' subsp. ''berlandieri'' var. ''macrocalycium''
*''C. b.'' subsp. ''berlandieri'' var. ''sinuatum''
*''C. b.'' subsp. ''berlandieri'' var. ''zschackii'' (Zschack's goosefoot)
The extinct variety is well-documented, though it may represent more than one taxon:<ref name=":5" />
* ''C. b.'' subsp. ''jonesianum''
Additionally, the [[cultivar]]s of the ''C. b. nuttalliae'' subspecies are:<ref name=smith2006/><ref name=Wilson1979>{{citation| doi=10.2307/2442525| last=Wilson| first=Hugh D. |author2=Heiser, Charles B. Jr | title=The Origin and Evolutionary Relationships of 'Huauzontle' (''Chenopodium nuttalliae'' Safford), Domesticated Chenopod of Mexico| jstor=2442525| year=1979| journal=American Journal of Botany| volume=66| issue=2| pages=198–206}}</ref>
*'Huauzontle' - This cultivar is a more recent selection used in commercial cultivation for a [[broccoli]]-like crop. It is a "naked" variety and has a testa only 2-7&nbsp;μm thick (cf. human hair, which is about 100&nbsp;μm wide).
*'Chia' - Grown as a grain crop, this cultivar is declining and is cultivated only on a local level. It also has a very thin testa, though slightly thicker than the previous at 10-20&nbsp;μm.
*'Quelite' - This cultivar is cultivated for its [[spinach]]-like leaves.
The species is capable of [[Hybrid (biology)|hybridizing]] with the related introduced [[European continent|European]] ''[[Chenopodium album]]'', which it resembles, giving the hybrid ''C. × variabile'' Aellen.<ref name="floraalbum">{{Citation| last1 =Clemants| first1 =Steven E. |last2=Mosyakin |first2=Sergei L. | contribution =Chenopodium album| year =2004| title =Flora of North America | volume =4| page =296| contribution-url =http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=1&taxon_id=200006809| place=New York & Oxford| publisher =Oxford University Press}}</ref>

Indigenous people used pitseed goosefoot not only for [[subsistence]], but also for medicinal and [[preservative]] reasons.<ref name=":9" /> {{failed verification|date=March 2026}} ''Chenopodium berlandieri'' prevents [[intestinal parasites]] and has the capacity of preserving foods.<ref name=":9" /> {{failed verification|date=March 2026}} These qualities may explain why indigenous people kept cultivating ''Chenopodium berlandieri'' despite the large effort of harvesting its minuscule seeds.<ref name=":9">Robinson, Daniel Shelton, " Chenopodium berlandieri and the Cultural Origins of Agriculture in the Eastern Woodlands. Although cultivation disappeared in eastern North America, ''C. b.'' subsp''. nuttalliae'' continues to be cultivated as a domesticated crop in Mexico.<ref name=":4" /> Three varieties of the subspecies are grown as a pseudocereal, as a [[leaf vegetable]], and for its [[Panicle|broccoli-like flowering shoots]], respectively.<ref name=smith2006/><ref name=Wilson1979/>

The principal difference between wild and domesticated forms of C''henopodium'' is the thickness of the seed coat. In the domesticated varieties, due to [[selective pressure]]s during domestication, the testas are less than 20 [[micron]]s thick; the testas of wild chenopods are 40 to 60 microns thick.<ref name=":3" /><ref>Smith, Bruce D. (1995), ''The Emergence of Agriculture'', New York: Scientific American Library, p. 184</ref> This morphological characteristic is shared by the modern cultivated chenopod ''C. b.'' subsp''. nuttalliae'' and the archaeological specimens of ''C. b.'' ssp''. jonesianum.''<ref>{{Cite journal|last=Wilson|first=Hugh D.|date=1981-04-01|title=DomesticatedChenopodium of the Ozark Bluff Dwellers|journal=Economic Botany|volume=35|issue=2|pages=233–239|doi=10.1007/BF02858690|bibcode=1981EcBot..35..233W |s2cid=23606041|issn=0013-0001}}</ref> Genetic studies have shown that eastern North American and Mexican cultivated forms have considerable [[genetic distance]] between them.<ref name=":1" /> Despite the initial assumption of a single domestication event, consensus in the field now supports at least two independent domestication events in North America.<ref name=smith2006/> Similarly, ''C. berlandieri''<nowiki/>'s South American branch likely experienced at least two independent domestication events, both of which are called ''C. quinoa.''<ref>{{Cite journal|last1=Risi|first1=J. == Weed status ==
Members of the ''Chenopodium'' species have been implicated among the greatest [[Weed|weed threats]] to agriculture in North America and globally.<ref name=":02">{{cite book |last1=Heap |first1=Ian |title=Integrated Pest Management |date=2014 |isbn=978-94-007-7795-8 |pages=281–314 |chapter=Herbicide Resistant Weeds |doi=10.1007/978-94-007-7796-5_12}}</ref> This success can be attributed to their ability to survive across a range of environmental conditions due to a high reproductive capacity, variation in their dormancy and germination requirements, and abiotic stress tolerance.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Holm |first1=L.G. |url=https://www.cabi.org/isc/abstract/19782319336 |title=The World's Worst Weeds. Distribution and biology. |last2=Plucknett |first2=D.L. |last3=Pancho |first3=J.V. |last4=Herberger |first4=J.P. |date=1977 |publisher=University Press of Hawaii |isbn=978-0-8248-0295-0 |location=Honolulu, Hawaii USA}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Bajwaa |first1=A.A. |last2=Zulfiqar |first2=U. |last3=Sadia |first3=S. |last4=Bhowmik |first4=P. |last5=Chauhan |first5=B.S. |date=2019 |title=A global perspective on the biology, impact and management of Chenopodium album and Chenopodium murale: two troublesome agricultural and environmental weeds. |journal=Environ Sci Pollut Res Int. |volume=26 |issue=6 |pages=5357–5371 |bibcode=2019ESPR...26.5357B |doi=10.1007/s11356-018-04104-y |pmid=30623324 |s2cid=58622221}}</ref>

Importantly, the ''Amaranthaceae'' family is one of five weed families (along with ''[[Poaceae]]'', ''[[Asteraceae]]'', ''[[Brassicaceae]]'', and ''[[Amaranthaceae|Chenopodiaceae]]'') that represent only 50% of the world's principal weeds but account for approximately 70% of all cases of [[Herbicide|herbicide resistance]].<ref name=":02" /> Most research identifies European species ''[[Chenopodium album|C. album]]'' as a prime candidate for resistance to multiple herbicides, in particular to [[triazine]]s and [[Glyphosate-based herbicides|glyphosates]]. The weed status and herbicide tolerance of ''C. berlandieri'' is less researched and less clear due to its many wild and semi-domesticated forms resulting from frequent hybridization and polyploidy.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Wilson |first1=Hugh D. |date=1980 |title=Artificial Hybridization Among Species of Chenopodium Sect. Chenopodium |journal=Systematic Botany |volume=5 |issue=3 |pages=253–263 |doi=10.2307/2418372 |jstor=2418372 |bibcode=1980SysBo...5..253W }}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Ohri |first1=D. |date=2015 |title=The taxonomic riddle of Chenopodium album L. complex (Amaranthaceae) |journal=Nucleus |volume=58 |issue=2 |pages=131–134 |doi=10.1007/s13237-015-0143-2 |s2cid=12855835}}</ref>

The spread and sporadic domestication of ''C. berlandieri'' across eastern [[North America|North]] and [[Central America]] has resulted in a complex network of domesticated and wild sub-species known to co-exist and interact in shared ecosystems. Human [[paleofeces]] collected from [[Salts Cave Archeological Site|Salts Cave]] in [[Kentucky]] and [[Bone Cave, Tennessee|Big Bone Cave]] in [[Tennessee]] were found to contain both seeds from weed and crop forms of the plant seemingly consumed within hours of each other, suggesting close spatial proximity and a potential for hybridization between populations.<ref name=":12">{{cite journal |last1=Gremillion |first1=Kristen J. |date=1993 |title=Crop and Weed in Prehistoric Eastern North America: The Chenopodium Example |journal=American Antiquity |volume=58 |issue=3 |pages=496–509 |doi=10.2307/282109 |jstor=282109 |bibcode=1993AmAnt..58..496G |s2cid=161993446}}</ref>

Morphological studies identified that seeds from weedy varieties of ''C. berlandieri'' tend to have a thicker testa (seed coat), a more rounded or biconvex margin configuration, more prominent testa patterning, a less developed beak, and a smaller overall size when compared to their domesticated counterparts. However, intermediate morphologies were also identified, indicating genetic interaction (crossing over) between these groups.<ref name=":12" />

This cross-compatibility and hybridization leads to the formation of crop-weed complexes, between ''C. berlandieri'' plants as well as with other members of the ''Chenopodium'' species.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Eslami |first1=Seyed Vahid |url=https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/B9780128229170000094 |title=Biology and Management of Problematic Crop Weed Species: Chenopodium album and Chenopodium murale |last2=Ward |first2=Sarah |date=2021 |publisher=Academic Press |isbn=978-0-12-822917-0 |pages=89–112}}</ref> For example, following the spread of ''C. quinoa'' across North America as a novel crop, one study found that up to 30% of wild ''C. berlandieri'' grown along the periphery of quinoa fields were crop/weed hybrids. [[Gene flow]] was observed to be asymmetric (from crop to weed), due to a preferential flow of pollen from high-density populations of domesticated ''C. quinoa'' to dispersed populations of wild ''C. berlandieri''.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Wilson |first1=H. |last2=Manhart |first2=J. |date=1993 |title=Crop/weed gene flow: Chenopodium quinoa Willd. and C. berlandieri Moq. |journal=Theoretical and Applied Genetics |volume=86 |issue=5 |pages=642–648 |doi=10.1007/BF00838721 |pmid=24193715 |s2cid=6123787}}</ref> This directional crop-weed interaction has implications for the future of introgressive change in wild ''C. berlandieri'' varieties. While [[Introgression|genetic introgression]] is often degenerative for both crops and wild plants,<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Jenczewski |first1=Eric |last2=Ronfort |first2=Joëlle |last3=Chèvre |first3=Anne-Marie |date=2003 |title=Crop-to-wild gene flow, introgression and possible fitness effects of transgenes |journal=Environmental Biosafety Research |volume=2 |issue=1 |pages=9–24 |doi=10.1051/ebr:2003001 |pmid=15615064 |doi-access=free}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Darmency |first1=H. |date=1994 |title=The impact of hybrids between genetically modified crop plants and their related species: introgression and weediness |journal=Molecular Ecology |volume=3 |issue=1 |pages=37–40 |bibcode=1994MolEc...3...37D |doi=10.1111/j.1365-294X.1994.tb00040.x |s2cid=85993512}}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |last1=Mueller |first1=Natalie G. |date=2017 |title=Growing the lost crops of eastern North America's original agricultural system |journal=Nature Plants |volume=3 |issue=7 |article-number=17092 |bibcode=2017NatPl...317092M |doi=10.1038/nplants.2017.92 |pmid=28696428 |s2cid=24538022}}</ref> it may also promote greater biodiversity in conventional cropping systems and present research opportunities for new crop varieties.

== Cultivation ==

=== Climate & soil requirements ===
''Chenopodium berlandieri'' is an extremely versatile plant; it can handle a variety of elevations, commonly found growing at sea level and at {{convert|10,000|ft|m}} elevation, such as in the [[San Juan Mountains|San Juan mountains]] of [[Colorado]]. In the [[Andes]] of [[South America]], there are varieties of lambsquarter that grow at over {{convert|12,000|ft|m}}.<ref name=":10">{{Cite book|last=Blair|first=Katrina|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=daBuBAAAQBAJ&q=the+wild+wisdom+of+weeds|title=The Wild Wisdom of Weeds: 13 Essential Plants for Human Survival|date=2014|publisher=Chelsea Green Publishing|isbn=978-1-60358-516-3|language=en}}</ref> It is very cold hardy and therefore one of the later weeds to be killed by [[frost]]. It dislikes shade. When exposed to full sun conditions, the plants tend to be robust with many lateral branches producing high quantities of seed. Plants growing in shaded conditions tend to be more gracile, taller, with fewer lateral branches and produce less seed.<ref>{{cite thesis|type=PhD thesis|last1=Halwas|first1=Sara Jane|date=2017|title=Domesticating Chenopodium: Applying Genetic Techniques and Archaeological Data to Understanding Pre-contact Plant Use in Southern Manitoba (AD1000-1500).|url=https://mspace.lib.umanitoba.ca/bitstream/handle/1993/32414/Halwas_Sara.pdf?sequence=1|publisher=University of Manitoba|access-date=14 November 2021}}</ref>

''Chenopodium berlandieri'' thrives in many types of [[soil]] with varying [[pH]] levels. When the soil is fertile, it will grow large and full in size and form very attractive stands of vegetation. The presence of a stand of healthy lambsquarter is one of the best indicators for vital soil. However, it can also handle the worst of soils and has been known to even survive in disturbed soils such as annual vegetable gardens, neglected fields and coal-pit heaps. Like its close relatives, it can serve as a [[cover crop]] and natural [[fertilizer]] because of its dense nutrient content.<ref name=":10" />

=== Sowing ===
C. Berlandieri is a self-seeding [[Annual vs. perennial plant evolution|annual]] plant. It grows easily from seed and does not require orderly cultivation.<ref>{{Cite web|title=Goosefoot {{!}} The Office of the State Archaeologist|url=https://archaeology.uiowa.edu/goosefoot|access-date=2021-11-18|website=archaeology.uiowa.edu}}</ref> The seeds themselves can stay dormant for many years and take root when the conditions are ideal. The species is [[Hermaphrodite|hermaphroditic]], having both male and female organs on the same plant, which are [[Anemophily|wind-pollinated]]. It is known to cross-pollinate with [[Chenopodium album]] to create a hybrid. The plant is in flower from July to October, with green-hued flowers. From August to October, the seeds ripen.<ref name=":10" />

=== Harvesting ===
''C. berlandieri'' is an elusive subject for harvest yield experiments; the floodplain weeds with their minuscule seeds are difficult to harvest relative to other species. High costs are associated with its harvesting due to the minute size and oiliness of seeds. Although occurring in vast numbers, seed size makes collecting enough for daily or long-term subsistence needs of an individual or group challenging. The relative cost of procurement and processing in quantities sufficient for a meal has been a limiting factor in their use throughout history and domestication has had little impact on reducing overall handling costs.<ref name=":9" />

=== Yield ===
The yield of pitseed goosefoot can vary substantially due to the differences in amount of sunlight received by the plants.<ref name=":6" /> Moreover, [[competition]] with surrounding plants can also influence how much yield is obtained.<ref name=":6" /> Studies have recorded yields between 276 and 2854&nbsp;kg/ha and estimate that the [[harvest]] yield of goosefoot in [[prehistoric times]] must have been around 750–1500&nbsp;kg/ha.<ref name=":6" /> A yield above 1000&nbsp;kg/ha must have been necessary to justify its use compared to [[maize]].<ref name=":6" /> Additionally, the [[harvest]] rate of pitseed goosefoot is 1&nbsp;kg/hour.<ref name=":2">{{cite journal|last=Gremillion|first=Kristen J.|date=2004|title=Seed Processing and the Origins of Food Production in Eastern North America|journal=American Antiquity|volume=69|issue=2|pages=215–233|doi=10.2307/4128417|jstor=4128417|bibcode=2004AmAnt..69..215G |s2cid=144789286|issn=0002-7316}}</ref>

== Nutrition ==
As with other [[Amaranthaceae]] species, ''C. berlandieri'' is rich in [[macronutrients]] of proteins, carbohydrates and fats, as well as [[micronutrient]]s including vitamins and minerals.<ref name=":3b">{{Cite journal|last1=Tang|first1=Yao|last2=Tsao|first2=Rong|date=2017|title=Phytochemicals in quinoa and amaranth grains and their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and potential health beneficial effects: a review|url=https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/mnfr.201600767|journal=Molecular Nutrition & Food Research|volume=61|issue=7|article-number=1600767|doi=10.1002/mnfr.201600767|pmid=28239982|issn=1613-4133|url-access=subscription}}</ref> Its high nutritional quality has given rise to researching its use for [[food security]] in rural populations.<ref name=":4b">{{Cite journal|last1=Santiago-Saenz|first1=Yair O.|last2=Hernández-Fuentes|first2=Alma D.|last3=Monroy-Torres|first3=Rebeca|last4=Cariño-Cortés|first4=Raquel|last5=Jiménez-Alvarado|first5=Rubén|date=2018-12-01|title=Physicochemical, nutritional and antioxidant characterization of three vegetables (Amaranthus hybridus L., Chenopodium berlandieri L., Portulaca oleracea L.) as potential sources of phytochemicals and bioactive compounds|journal=Journal of Food Measurement and Characterization|language=en|volume=12|issue=4|pages=2855–2864|doi=10.1007/s11694-018-9900-7|s2cid=105289484|issn=2193-4134}}</ref>

The leaf nutritional content, expressed per 100 g of fresh weight: 0.2 kJ Fat, 3.45 kJ Protein, 3.17 kJ total dietary fiber (primarily insoluble fiber; 8.3% to 12.8% of the [[recommended daily intake]]), 111.8 kJ energy, 2.21 kJ available carbohydrates.<ref name=":4b" /> The leaves are sources of [[phytochemical]]s and nutritional compounds.<ref name=":4b" /> They have shown significant contents of protein, [[Inorganic compound|inorganic]] nutrients of Calcium (Ca), Iron (Fe) and Magnesium (Mg).<ref name=":4b" /> It also has a high percentage of [[Oleic acid|oleic]], [[Linoleic acid|linoleic]] and [[linolenic acid]]s, which are essential for human nutrition,<ref>{{Citation|last1=de la Cruz Torres|first1=Eulogio|chapter=The Genus Chenopodium: A Potential Food Source|date=2013|title=Biotechnology of Neglected and Underutilized Crops|pages=3–31|editor-last=Jain|editor-first=Shri Mohan|place=Dordrecht|publisher=Springer Netherlands|language=en|doi=10.1007/978-94-007-5500-0_1|isbn=978-94-007-5500-0 |last2=Palomino Hasbach|first2=Guadalupe|last3=García Andrade|first3=Juan Manuel|last4=Mapes Sánchez|first4=Cristina|last5=González Jiménez|first5=Josefina|last6=Falcón Bárcenas|first6=Thelma|last7=Vázquez Arriaga|first7=Octavio|editor2-last=Dutta Gupta|editor2-first=S.}}</ref> and highest total [[flavonoid]]s index (TFI) when compared to other [[Amaranthaceae|Amaranthacae]] species.<ref name=":4b" />

The plant is a good source of fiber and has high [[flavonoid]]s concentration, such as [[quercetin]] and [[kaempferol]], which have high [[antioxidant]] potential.<ref name=":4b" /> Leaves have been analyzed to contain higher [[chlorophyll]] content compared to other [[Amaranthaceae|Amaranthacae]] species, which is nutritionally relevant, as it has been reported to reduce [[reactive oxygen species]].<ref name=":4b" />

The seeds are [[gluten]] free.<ref name=":3b" /> Like other [[quinoa]] and [[amaranth]] species, proteins in the seeds are of particularly high nutritional value due to high concentration of [[essential amino acid]]s.<ref name=":3b" /> Safety concerns have been raised around [[saponin]]s, which are toxic, though mostly to fish.<ref name=":1b">{{Cite web |title=Chenopodium berlandieri Southern Huauzontle, Pitseed goosefoot, Nuttall's goosefoot, Bush's goosefoot, Zschack's goosefoot PFAF Plant Database |url=https://pfaf.org/user/Plant.aspx?LatinName=Chenopodium+berlandieri |access-date=2021-11-13 |website=pfaf.org}}</ref> In the plant, the [[saponin]] quantity is too small to harm humans.<ref name=":1b" /> Studies have shown that some [[saponin]]s may form insoluble complexes with minerals, such as zinc and iron, thus negatively affecting absorption and [[bioavailability]] of nutrients in the gut.<ref name=":3b" /> Saponins are bitter, but break down during the cooking process, rendering them harmless and allowing nutrients to be [[Bioavailability|bioavailable]] to humans.<ref name=":1b" /> Cooking also reduces the [[oxalic acid]] content, which may also raise concerns.<ref name=":1b" />

== Uses ==
''Chenopodium berlandieri'' has little presence in the current world food system, especially compared to other plants within its genus, such as ''[[Quinoa|Chenopodium quinoa]]'' or ''[[Dysphania ambrosioides|C. ambrosioides]]''. This may be due to a recent Western bias against weedy plants, as well as a manifestation of colonial history which led to a disconnect from the local environment and indigenous knowledge.<ref name=":9" />

Today, the plant is still used as an edible herb and vegetable, primarily in [[Mexico]]. Edible parts of the plant include leaves, young [[Shoot (botany)|shoot]]s and seeds. Like other leafy greens, the leaves and shoots are eaten raw or cooked,<ref name=":1b" /> though raw leaves are bitter as they contain chemical compounds produced by the plant for defense mechanisms.

The plant can be processed through mechanical and chemical techniques.<ref name=":2" /> Mechanical processing techniques include [[winnowing]] to remove non-nutritive components, including possibly toasting the seeds during the winnowing process, followed by [[Gristmill|milling]] to de-husk the plant and separate the seeds.<ref name=":2" />

The seeds can then be ground down into smaller particles to make [[grits]] or [[bulgur]], or ground further into flours to be combined with cereal [[flour]]s to make [[bread]]s or [[pancake]]s.<ref name=":1b" />

The seeds contain [[saponin]]s, though in small quantities, which are removed by soaking seeds in water overnight and rinsing thoroughly before further processing.<ref name=":1b" /> Another method involves chemical processing, whereby gradual, controlled heating of the seeds detoxifies them, allowing nutrients to be bioavailable for digestion.<ref name=":2" /> People with [[gout]], [[arthritis]], [[rheumatism]], [[Kidney stone disease|kidney stones]], or [[hyperacidity]] should use caution when consuming the plant, as it may aggravate their condition.<ref name=":1b" />

=== Medicinal potential ===
''Chenopodium berlandieri'' is a prime candidate for additional research, especially its prospects for utility in the medicinal model. Long-term use of this plant makes little sense when judged in strict terms of subsistence. ''C.&nbsp;berlandieri'' shares some qualities with its medicinally useful relatives such as ''[[Dysphania ambrosioides|Chenopodium ambrosioides]]'' which is a widely known [[vermifuge]] and a potential [[Food preservation|food preservative]]. This makes its potential medicinal value a possibility. Its chemical constituents and possible medicinal properties have only been briefly examined in the [[Anthropology|anthropological]] and [[Botany|botanical]] literature. The useful substances in the leaves avoid the problems associated with the inefficiency of harvesting and processing the seeds.<ref>{{cite journal|last1=Robinson|first1=Daniel Shelton|title=Chenopodium berlandieri and the Cultural Origins of Agriculture in the Eastern Woodlands|url=https://trace.tennessee.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2255&context=utk_gradthes|journal=Masters Thesis, University of Tennessee, Knoxville|access-date=14 November 2021}}</ref> Tests for and analyses of [[Biological activity|bioactive]] chemical compounds would likely resolve questions about the use of ''Chenopodium berlandieri'' and the medicinal use may be evident in the concentration of these compounds.
