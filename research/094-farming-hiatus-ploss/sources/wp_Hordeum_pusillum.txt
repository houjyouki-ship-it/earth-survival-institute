'''''Hordeum pusillum''''', also known as '''little barley''', is an [[Annual plant|annual]] grass native to most of the [[United States]] and southwestern [[Canada]].<ref name=":1">{{Cite web |title=USDA Plants Database |url=https://plants.usda.gov/home/plantProfile?symbol=HOPU |access-date=2022-06-20 |website=plants.usda.gov}}</ref>{{r|BSBI07}} It arrived via multiple long-distance [[Biological dispersal|dispersal]]s of a southern [[South America]]n species of ''[[Hordeum]]'' about one million years ago.{{r|blattner2006}} Its closest relatives are therefore not the other North American [[Taxon|taxa]] like [[Hordeum brachyantherum|meadow barley]] (''Hordeum brachyantherum'') or [[Hordeum jubatum|foxtail barley]] (''Hordeum jubatum''), but rather ''Hordeum'' species of the [[Pampas]] of central [[Argentina]] and [[Uruguay]]. It is less closely related to the [[Old World]] domesticated [[barley]], from which it diverged about 12 million years ago. It is [[ploidy#Diploid|diploid]].{{r|pleines2008}}

== Etymology ==
[[Hordeum]] comes from the Latin word horreō, horrēre "to bristle " and ''[[wiktionary:pusillum|pusillum]]'' is the "nominative neuter singular of [[wiktionary:pusillus#Latin|pusillus"]]- "very little, very small, tiny."

== Description ==
First described in 1818 by [[Thomas Nuttall]],<ref name=":13">{{Cite book |last=Minnis |first=Paul E. |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=5fQrEAAAQBAJ&dq=hordeum+pusillum&pg=PA139 |title=New Lives for Ancient and Extinct Crops |date=September 2016 |publisher=University of Arizona Press |isbn=978-0-8165-3422-7 |language=en}}</ref> ''Hordeum'' ''pusillum'', also known as little barley, is an annual [[flowering plant]] native to the majority of North America, mainly the United States and southwestern Canada.<ref name=":1" /> It is a member of the subfamily [[Pooideae]] in the grass family [[Poaceae]]. The plant itself is approximately {{cvt|14–40|cm}} tall and is self-fertilizing.<ref name=":13" /> Leaves and spikelets are alternate. The flat, pubescent leaves can range anywhere from {{cvt|2.4–12.7|cm}} in length and {{cvt|2.0–4.5|mm}} wide. The sheath of little barley can be either glabrous or pubescent and wraps loosely around the stem.<ref name=":3">{{Cite web |title=Hordeum pusillum (Little Barley): Minnesota Wildflowers |url=https://www.minnesotawildflowers.info/grass-sedge-rush/little-barley |access-date=2021-12-01 |website=www.minnesotawildflowers.info |language=en}}</ref> The inflorescence ranges between {{cvt|4–8|cm}} long. Of the three alternating floral spikelets, only one is fertile. The plant's growth period is during the winter months, producing mature grains by April.<ref name=":13" /> The roots are fibrous, and the mark of a mature spikelet is when they turn a tan to brown color. The stem changes from a bluish-green color to brown as the plant matures.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Little Barley // Mizzou WeedID |url=https://weedid.missouri.edu/weedinfo.cfm?weed_id=130 |access-date=2021-12-01 |website=weedid.missouri.edu}}</ref> Its habitat is in sunny locations on dry gravely soils, rock outcrops, roadsides, railroads, waste places, in grasslands, and on marsh edges.<ref name=":3" />

== Germination ==
Little barley germinates best when exposed to light, and experiences best germination after one to two weeks of pre-chilling. Anymore than that and the viability of seeds decreases over time. After two weeks of pre-chilling, little barley seeds go into dormancy, allowing little barley to overwinter and come back year after year. Seeds germinate best between 17 and 20 degrees C. The more mature the seeds, the better they germinate.<ref>{{Cite journal|last1=Fischer|first1=M. Leon|last2=Stritzke|first2=J. F.|last3=Ahring|first3=Robert M.|date=1982|title=Germination and Emergence of Little Barley (Hordeum pusillum)|url=https://www.jstor.org/stable/4043656|journal=Weed Science|volume=30|issue=6|pages=624–628|doi=10.1017/S0043174500041321|jstor=4043656|s2cid=88806555 |issn=0043-1745|url-access=subscription}}</ref>

== Pollination ==
Pollination occurs during the winter through the summer.<ref name=":2">{{Cite web|title=Little Barley (Hordeum pusillum) Species Details and Allergy Info|url=https://www.pollenlibrary.com/Specie/Hordeum+pusillum/|access-date=2021-12-01|website=www.pollenlibrary.com}}</ref> The center spikelet is fertile whilst the lateral spikelets are male and infertile.<ref name=":13" />

== Weed control ==
Little barley can suppress the growth of more desirable forage grasses. Little barley is best controlled prior to its dormancy in the fall or early spring, and can be further controlled through the use of the herbicides. Spring usage of herbicides has also been found effective at controlling the growth of little barley. Late winter or early spring treatment with Accent has been found effective. Prolonged usage of herbicides can result in higher tolerance.<ref>{{Cite web|title=Weed of the Month: Carolina Foxtail and Little Barley|url=https://ipm.missouri.edu/ipcm/2015/3/Weed-of-the-Month-Carolina-Foxtail-and-Little-Barley/|access-date=2021-12-01|website=ipm.missouri.edu|language=en}}</ref>[[File:Hordeum_pusillum_catu.jpg|thumb|''Hordeum'' ''pusillum'' herbarium sheet]]

== Domestication ==
Little barley is believed to have been cultivated by Native Americans due to its abundance in archaeological sites. Evidence for the earliest known cultivation in eastern North America was found at the Gast Spring site in what is today [[Louisa County, Iowa]]. Seeds were found alongside domesticated [[Chenopodium|goosefoot]] seeds and [[Cucurbita|squash or gourd]] rinds dating to 2,800 to 3,000 years ago. Large plots were required to produce adequate harvests due to the grain's small size. ''Hordeum'' ''pusillum'' was briefly domesticated during the [[Pre-Columbian era|Prehispanic period]]. Evidence suggests domestication took place in the southeastern and southwestern United States. In the southeastern and midwestern United States, however, domestication lasted through the Middle Archaic and protohistoric periods.<ref name=":13"/> To the [[Hohokam]] culture in [[Arizona]], archeological evidence suggests that little barley was used for trade between other tribes whose diet did not normally include domesticated little barley.<ref name=":13"/>

Little barley cultivation is important in understanding pre-maize agriculture.<ref name=":22">{{Cite web |title=Little Barley {{!}} The Office of the State Archaeologist |url=https://archaeology.uiowa.edu/little-barley |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141130212909/http://archaeology.uiowa.edu/little-barley |url-status=dead |archive-date=November 30, 2014 |access-date=2021-12-01 |website=archaeology.uiowa.edu }}</ref>

==Uses==
The small grains are edible, and this plant was part of the [[Eastern Agricultural Complex]] of cultivated plants used in the Pre-Columbian era by [[Native Americans in the United States|Native Americans]].<ref>{{Cite journal |last1=VanDerwarker |first1=Amber M. |last2=Bardolph |first2=Dana N. |last3=Hoppa |first3=Kristin M. |last4=Thakar |first4=Heather B. |last5=Martin |first5=Lana S. |last6=Jaqua |first6=Allison L. |last7=Biwer |first7=Matthew E. |last8=Gill |first8=Kristina M. |date=2016-06-01 |title=New World Paleoethnobotany in the New Millennium (2000–2013) |url=https://doi.org/10.1007/s10814-015-9089-9 |journal=Journal of Archaeological Research |language=en |volume=24 |issue=2 |pages=138 |doi=10.1007/s10814-015-9089-9 |s2cid=146302660 |issn=1573-7756|url-access=subscription }}</ref> Before being displaced by [[agriculture]] based on [[maize]], little barley may have been [[Domestication#Domestication of plants|domesticated]].<ref name=":13" />

== Edibility ==
The grains would be dried, processed, and then cooked before eating.<ref name=":13"/> Cultivated for its edible grains, it is also classified as a [[cereal]] grain. Little barley seeds have an awn, a sharp hair-like attachment on the grain, which was then separated from the grain and possibly parched, roasted, and boiled. The seeds are nutritious and starchy.<ref name=":22"/> 100 grams of little barley constitutes almost 24.3 percent of carbohydrates, 22.4 percent of protein, 18 percent of calories, and 5–6 percent of fiber and fat of a recommended daily 2,000-calorie diet.<ref name=":13" />

== Allergenic ==
Little barley is known to be a mild allergen.<ref name=":2" />
