[[Image:Motte Oakham.jpg|thumb|right| Remains of a Norman [[motte and bailey]] castle at the lost village of [[Alstoe]], in [[Rutland]], England]]

In the United Kingdom, a '''deserted medieval village''' ('''DMV''') is a [[abandoned village|former settlement]] which was abandoned during the [[Middle Ages]], typically leaving no trace apart from [[Earthworks (archaeology)|earthworks]] or [[cropmark]]s.{{cn|date=September 2020}} If there are three or fewer inhabited houses the convention is to regard the site as deserted; if there are more than three houses, it is regarded as a '''shrunken medieval village'''. There are estimated to be more than 3,000 DMVs in England alone.

== Other deserted settlements ==
Not all sites are medieval: villages reduced in size or disappeared over a long period, from as early as [[Anglo-Saxon England|Anglo-Saxon]] times to as late as the 1960s, due to numerous different causes.

== Reasons for desertion ==
Over the centuries, settlements have been deserted as a result of natural events, such as rivers changing course or [[silting up]], flooding (especially during the wet 13th and 14th centuries) as well as coastal and estuarine [[erosion]] or being overwhelmed by windblown sand.

[[Image:The site of the medieval village of Buslingthorpe in Lincolnshire.jpg|right|thumb|A [[lidar]] view of the medieval village of Buslingthorpe in Lincolnshire.]]

Many were thought to have been abandoned due to the deaths of their inhabitants from the [[Black Death]] in the mid-14th century. While the [[Bubonic plague|plague]] would often have greatly hastened the population decline, which had already set in by the early 14th century in England because of [[soils retrogression and degradation|soil exhaustion]] and disease,{{cn|date=December 2021}} most DMVs actually seem to have become deserted during the 15th century. At this time, {{notatypo|[[inclosure act]]s}} and other policies allowed land traditionally cultivated for [[cereal]]s and vegetables to be transformed into [[pasture]]s for sheep. The medieval [[ridge and furrow]] cultivation pattern remains evident in fields, even until today. This change of [[land use]] by landowners, which was to take advantage of the profitable wool trade, led to hundreds of villages being deserted.

Later, the aristocratic fashion for grand country [[mansion]]s, parks and landscaped gardens led to whole villages being moved or destroyed to enable [[lords of the manor]] to participate in this trend, a process often called [[enclosure|emparkment]] or [[enclosure]].

[[File:A lidar view of Halton in Northumberland south of Hunnum Roman fort.jpg|thumb|A [[lidar]] view of Halton shrunken medieval village in Northumberland south of [[Hunnum]] Roman fort]]
[[File:Sudeley Castle with early Medieval manor house and field system and early to Medieval deserted settlement.jpg|thumb|A [[lidar]] view of [[Sudeley Castle]] with the site of an early Medieval manor house and field system and early to Medieval deserted settlement]]

Perhaps the best-known deserted medieval village in England is at [[Wharram Percy]] in [[North Yorkshire]], because of the extensive [[Archaeology|archaeological]] excavations conducted there between its discovery in 1948 and 1990. Its ruined church and its former fishpond are still visible.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://pastscape.english-heritage.org.uk/hob.aspx?hob_id=62053|archive-url=https://archive.today/20120715045840/http://pastscape.english-heritage.org.uk/hob.aspx?hob_id=62053|url-status=dead|archive-date=2012-07-15|title=National Monument record for Wharram Percy}}</ref>

In [[Northamptonshire]], around 100 villages can be classified as deserted: there are articles relating to many of them, such as [[Onley (lost settlement)|Onley]], [[Althorp (lost settlement)|Althorp]], [[Canons Ashby (lost settlement)|Canons Ashby]], [[Church Charwelton (lost settlement)|Church Charwelton]] and [[Coton, Northamptonshire (lost settlement)|Coton]] along with [[Faxton]], [[Glendon (lost settlement)|Glendon]], [[Snorscombe]], [[Wolfhampcote]] and [[Wythmail]].

Other examples are at [[Gainsthorpe]] and [[Burreth, Lincolnshire|Burreth]] in [[Lincolnshire]].<ref>{{cite web|url=http://pastscape.english-heritage.org.uk/hob.aspx?hob_id=63472|archive-url=https://archive.today/20120718230521/http://pastscape.english-heritage.org.uk/hob.aspx?hob_id=63472|url-status=dead|archive-date=2012-07-18|title=National Monument record for Gainsthorpe}}</ref><ref>{{cite book|first=Maurice|last=Beresford| author-link = Maurice Beresford |year=1983|title=Lost Villages of England|pages=945, 98, 265, 335, 363 }}</ref><ref>{{cite web |last1=Historic England |title=Burreth |url=https://www.pastscape.org.uk/hob.aspx?hob_id=351294&sort=4&search=all&criteria=Burreth&rational=q&recordsperpage=10 |website=Pastscape |access-date=6 May 2019 |archive-url=https://archive.today/20190505115304/https://www.pastscape.org.uk/hob.aspx?hob_id=351294&sort=4&search=all&criteria=Burreth&rational=q&recordsperpage=10 |archive-date=5 May 2019 |date=2015 |url-status=live }}</ref>
