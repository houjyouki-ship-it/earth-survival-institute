'''Greenlandic Norse''' is an extinct [[North Germanic languages|North Germanic language]] that was spoken in the [[Norse settlements of Greenland]] until their demise in the late 15th century. The language is primarily attested by runic inscriptions found in [[Greenland]]. The limited inscriptional evidence shows some innovations, including the use of initial ''t'' for ''[[Voiceless dental fricative|þ]]'', but also the conservation of certain features that changed in other Norse languages. Some runic features are regarded as characteristically Greenlandic, and when they are sporadically found outside of Greenland, they may suggest travelling Greenlanders.

Non-runic evidence on the Greenlandic language is scarce and uncertain. A document issued in Greenland in 1409 is preserved in an Icelandic copy and may be a witness to some Greenlandic linguistic traits. The poem {{lang|non|[[Atlamál]]}} is credited as ''Greenlandic'' in the [[Codex Regius]], but the preserved text reflects Icelandic scribal conventions, and it is not certain that the poem was composed in Greenland. Greenlandic Norse is believed to have been in [[language contact]] with [[Greenlandic language|Greenlandic]] and to have left loanwords in it.

==Runic evidence==
[[File:Runesten fra Kingittorsuaq.jpg|thumb|The Kingittorsuaq Runestone]]
Some 80 [[runic inscriptions]] have been found in Greenland. Many of them are difficult to date and not all of them were necessarily carved by Greenlanders.<ref name='Hagland'>Hagland, p. 1234.</ref> It is difficult to identify specifically Greenlandic linguistic features in the limited runic material. Nevertheless, there are inscriptions showing the use of ''t'' for historical ''[[þ]]'' in words such as {{lang|non-GL|torir}} rather than {{lang|non|þorir}} and {{lang|non-GL|tana}} rather than {{lang|non|þana}}. This linguistic innovation has parallels in West Norwegian in the [[late Middle Ages|late medieval period]].<ref name='Hagland'/> On the other hand, Greenlandic appears to have retained some features which changed in other types of Scandinavian. This includes initial ''hl'' and ''hr'', otherwise only preserved in [[Icelandic language|Icelandic]], and the long vowel ''œ'' (''oe'' ligature), which merged with ''æ'' (''ae'' ligature) in Icelandic but was preserved in [[Old Norwegian|Norwegian]] and [[Faroese language|Faroese]].<ref>Barnes 2005, p. 185.</ref>

Certain runic forms have been seen by scholars as characteristically Greenlandic, including in particular an 'r' form with two parallel sloping branches{{mdash}}{{thinspace}}[[File:Greenlandic r rune.svg|frameless|x12px]]{{thinspace}}{{mdash}}which is found in 14 Greenlandic inscriptions.<ref>Stoklund, p. 535.</ref> This form is sporadically found outside Greenland. It is, for example, found in a runic inscription discovered in [[Orphir]] in Orkney, which has been taken to imply that "the rune carver probably was a Greenlander".<ref>Liestøl, p. 236.</ref>

The [[Kingittorsuaq Runestone]] has one of the longest Norse inscriptions found in Greenland. It was discovered near [[Upernavik]], far north of the Norse settlements. It was presumably carved by Norse explorers. Like most Greenlandic inscriptions, it is traditionally dated to {{circa|1300}}. However, Marie Stoklund has called for reconsideration of the dating of the Greenlandic material and points out that some of the parallels to the Kingittorsuaq inscription elsewhere in the Nordic world have been dated to {{circa|1200}}.<ref>Stoklund, p. 534.</ref>

The patronymic {{lang|non-GL|Tortarson}} ([[Old Norse orthography#Normalized spelling|standardized Old Norse]]: {{lang|non|Þórðarson}}) shows the change from ''þ'' to ''t'' while the word {{lang|non-GL|hloþu}} (Old Icelandic {{lang|non-IS|hlóðu}}, Old Norwegian {{lang|non-NO|lóðu}}) shows the retention of initial ''hl''.

==Manuscript evidence==
A document written at [[Garðar, Greenland|Garðar]] in Greenland in 1409 is preserved in an Icelandic transcription from 1625. The transcription was attested by bishop {{ill|Oddur Einarsson|is}} and is considered reliable. The document is a marriage certificate issued by two priests based in Greenland, attesting the [[banns of marriage]] for two Icelanders who had been [[blown off course]] to Greenland, Þorsteinn Ólafsson and Sigríður Björnsdóttir. The language of the document is clearly not Icelandic and cannot without reservation be classified as Norwegian. It may have been produced by Norwegian-educated clergy who had been influenced by Greenlandic.<ref>Olsen, pp. 236&ndash;237.</ref> The document contains orthographic traits which are consistent with the runic linguistic evidence. This includes the prepositional form ''þil'' for the older ''til'' which demonstrates the merger of initial 'þ' and 't'.<ref>Olsen, p. 245.</ref>
[[File:Beginning of Atlamál.jpg|thumb|350x350px|The introduction of ''Atlamál'' in the Codex Regius: "E''nn'' segir gle''gg''ra í Atlamalo''m'' enum grǫnlenzcom",<ref>Bugge, p. 291.</ref> – "Yet more fully is spoken (of this) in "The Greenlandish Lay of Atli.""<ref>Hollander, p. 293.</ref>]]
It is possible that some other texts preserved in Icelandic manuscripts might be of Greenlandic origins. In particular, the poem ''[[Atlamál]]'' is referred to as Greenlandic (''Atlamál in grœnlenzku'') in the [[Codex Regius]]. Many scholars have understood the reference to mean that the poem was composed by a Greenlander and various elements of the poem's text have been taken to support Greenlandic provenance. [[Ursula Dronke]] commented that "There is a rawness about the language ... that could reflect the conditions of an isolated society distant from the courts of kings and such refinements of manners and speech as were associated with them."<ref>Dronke, p. 108.</ref>

[[Finnur Jónsson]] argued that not only was ''Atlamál'' composed in Greenland, some other preserved Eddic poems were as well. He adduced various stylistic arguments in favor of Greenlandic provenance for ''[[Helgakviða Hundingsbana I]]'', ''[[Oddrúnargrátr]]'', ''[[Guðrúnarhvöt]]'', ''[[Sigurðarkviða in skamma]]'' and, more speculatively, ''[[Helreið Brynhildar]]''.<ref>Finnur Jónsson, p. 66–72.</ref> One linguistic trait which Finnur regarded as specifically Greenlandic was initial 'hn' in the word ''[[Niflung|Hniflungr]]'', found in'' Atlamál'', ''Helgakviða Hundingsbana I'' and ''Guðrúnarhvöt''. The word is otherwise preserved as ''Niflungr'' in Icelandic sources.<ref>Finnur Jónsson, p. 71.</ref> Modern scholarship is doubtful of using ''Atlamál'' as a source on the Greenlandic language since its Greenlandic origin is not certain, it is difficult to date, and the preserved text reflects Icelandic scribal conventions.<ref>von See et al., pp. 387–390.</ref><ref>Barnes 2002, pp. 1054–1055.</ref>

==Contact with Kalaallisut==

Greenlandic Norse is believed to have been in [[language contact]] with [[Greenlandic language|Kalaallisut]], the language of the [[Kalaallit]], and to have left [[loanword]]s in that language. In particular, the Greenlandic word {{lang|kl|Kalaaleq}} (older {{lang|kl|Karaaleq}}), meaning ''Greenlander'', is believed to be derived from the word {{lang|non|[[Skræling]]r}}, the Norse term for the people they [[Norse colonization of the Americas|encountered in North America]].<ref>Jahr, p. 233.</ref> In the Greenlandic dictionary of 1750, [[Hans Egede]] states that {{lang|kl|Karálek}} is what the "old Christians" called the Greenlanders and that they use the word only with foreigners and not when speaking among themselves.<ref>{{lang|la|Ita vocatus se dictitant à priscis Christianis, terræ hujus qvondam incolis. Nostro ævo usurpatur duntaxat ab Advenis, Grœnlandiam invisentibus, ab indigenis non item.}} Egede 1750, p. 68.</ref><ref>Thalbitzer, p. 36.</ref> Other words which may be of Norse origin include {{lang|kl|Kuuna}} (female given name, probably from Old Norse {{lang|non|kona}} "woman", "wife"),<ref>{{Cite web|title=girls name Kuuna – Oqaasileriffik|url=https://oqaasileriffik.gl/resources/did-you-know/girls-name-kuuna/|access-date=7 March 2021|language=en-US}}</ref> {{lang|kl|sava}} ("sheep", Old Norse {{lang|non|sauðr}}), {{lang|kl|nisa}} ("porpoise", Old Norse {{lang|non|hnísa}}), {{lang|kl|puuluki}} ("pig", Old Norse {{lang|non|purka}} "sow"), {{lang|kl|musaq}} ("carrot", Old Norse {{lang|non|mura}}) and {{lang|kl|kuaneq}} ("[[angelica]]", Old Norse {{lang|non|hvönn}}, plural {{lang|non|hvannir}}).<ref>Jahr, p. 231.</ref><ref>Thalbitzer, pp. 35-36.</ref>

The available evidence does not establish the presence of [[language attrition]]; the Norse language most likely disappeared with the ethnic group that spoke it.<ref name='Hagland'/>
