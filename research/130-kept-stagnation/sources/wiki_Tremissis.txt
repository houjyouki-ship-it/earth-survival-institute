The '''tremissis''' or '''tremis''' ([[Byzantine Greek|Greek]]: τριμίσιον, ''trimision'') was a small pure gold coin of [[Late Antiquity]]. Its name, meaning "a third of a unit", formed by analogy with [[semissis]] (half of a unit), indicated its value relative to the [[Solidus (coin)|solidus]]. It was introduced into [[Roman currency]] in the 380s by the Emperor [[Theodosius I]] and initially weighed 8 [[siliquae]] (1.52 grams).<ref name=ODB>[[Philip Grierson]], "Tremissis", in [[Alexander Kazhdan]], ed., ''The Oxford Dictionary of Byzantium'' (Oxford University Press, 1991 [online 2005]), vol. 3, p. 2113.</ref>

Roman tremisses were commonly minted into the reign of [[Leo III the Isaurian|Leo III]] (717–741), but after that they were only rarely struck in the east of the empire, probably only for ceremonial uses, until the reign of [[Basil I]] (867–886), after which they disappeared. Nevertheless, the coin continued in common use in the [[Sicily (theme)|Sicilian theme]] until the fall of Syracuse in 878. The [[Trachy (currency)|trachy]], introduced in the 11th century, was equivalent in value to the old tremissis. Although it was not made of gold, it was one third of the standard golden [[hyperpyron]]. It was not, however, called tremissis.<ref name=ODB/>

Outside of the Roman empire, tremisses were minted by the [[Anglo-Saxons]], [[Burgundians]], [[Francia|Franks]], [[Frisian Kingdom|Frisians]], [[Kingdom of the Lombards|Lombards]], [[Ostrogothic Kingdom|Ostrogoths]], [[Suevic Kingdom of Galicia|Suevi]] and [[Visigothic Kingdom|Visigoths]] between the 5th and 8th centuries.<ref>"Tremissis", in Robert E. Bjork, ed., ''The Oxford Dictionary of the Middle Ages'' (Oxford University Press, 2010).</ref> The word tremissis was borrowed into [[Old English]] as ''[[thrymsa]]''.<ref>"Thrymsas", in Robert E. Bjork, ed., ''The Oxford Dictionary of the Middle Ages'' (Oxford University Press, 2010).</ref>

In Frankish sources, the tremissis is sometimes called a [[triens]], a term likewise meaning "a third", which originally referred to a bronze coin worth a third of an [[As (coin)|as]]. The historian and bishop [[Gregory of Tours]] calls the Frankish tremissis a ''trians'' or ''treans''. The [[Old High German|German]] form {{lang|de|dremise}} is also attested. In French historiography the term {{lang|fr|tiers}} (third) or {{lang|fr|tiers de sou}} (third of a solidus) is often used. The French, in general, prefer to call the coin of the [[Merovingian]] kings a triens (but avoiding the plural form ''trientes''), while British scholarship prefers tremissis.<ref>Philip Grierson and Mark Blackburn, ''Medieval European Coinage: Volume 1, The Early Middle Ages (5th–10th Centuries)'' (Cambridge University Press, 1986), p. 102.</ref>

It was still used as an accounting currency until at least the 12th century in Sardinia. It appears as ''tremisse'' in the ''[[condaghe]]''.<ref>Il condaghe di Santa Maria di Bonarcado / a cura di Maurizio Virdis. - Nuoro : Ilisso, 2003</ref>
