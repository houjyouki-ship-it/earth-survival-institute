1.1 Chair’s report
It is a privilege to present my first annual report as Chair of the National Library
of Australia Council.

Stories held within the National Library’s physical and digital worlds connect with my own
history in so many ways: Eualayai language and cultural material; my mother’s service
as a ‘WRAN’; the inside story of the legal institutions that have formed the basis of my
professional life; the personal archive I deposited with the National Library many years
ago, not imagining I would one day be its Chair, and of course the books I have written.

The National Library of Australia is indeed Treasured by the nation, as it pursues its long-
term purposes – to build an outstanding national collection, to provide access to that
collection, and to work with other organisations to amplify the value of Australia’s cultural
treasures to benefit the entire Australian community.

In recent years, the National Library has worked purposefully to ensure that all Australians
can – as I do – see themselves in the national collection.

Intensive outreach to and engagement with First Nations leaders and communities have
seen the National Library build its collections, engage with many more communities about
the Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property in the collections, and host a series of
high-quality, Indigenous-led public programs, drawing new onsite and online audiences.

Building on the success of earlier targeted engagement with Chinese–Australian and
Fijian–Australian communities, this year concluded a successful 2-year program to engage
the Indian diaspora, the fastest growing migrant community in Australia. As a result, the
collection has been enriched with oral histories, publications, photographs, websites and
archives – truly making the National Library a place for every story.

The National Library’s reach into the Australian community is wide as well as deep.

As the nation went to the polls in May, the National Library and the Australian community
worked together to ensure preservation of a wide range of 2025 federal election material
for future generations: websites, pamphlets, handbills, corflutes and more. This material
is of great interest to social historians and the general public, as our successful 2024
Tax Time Appeal demonstrated: thanks to community support, thousands of pieces of
historical election ephemera are now out of their National Library boxes and available
online via Trove, reflecting the strength of Australia’s democracy.

The National Library reflects the nation in good times and in bad. This year saw us
drawing to a close a large-scale oral history project that captured the experiences of 260
of the millions of Australians whose lives were disrupted by the COVID-19 pandemic. From
political, scientific and health leaders, to those trying to protect vulnerable communities,




2 NATIONAL LIBRARY OF AUSTRALIA                ANNUAL REPORT 2024–25
to the experiences of students completing their educations in bedrooms, parents
welcoming babies in lockdown, and grieving relatives unable to attend deathbeds or
funerals – this project will provide a wealth of material for future researchers.

The year has delivered increasing challenges for arts and culture organisations, including
libraries. The number of book challenges – threatening Australians’ freedom to read –
continues to rise. In addition, geopolitical tensions risk sharply dividing community views
on several issues, with issues that would once have been uncontroversial now highly
contested. In this environment, it is important that libraries continue to be a space of
learning, informed exchange of ideas and inclusion.

To maintain community trust, cultural organisations must review their risk frameworks
and appetites and be more prepared to respond to rapidly escalating political and media
commentary. The National Library is not immune from this challenge. The National
Library Council and Executive are working carefully and thoughtfully to prepare for such
challenges while not diminishing freedom of thought, of expression, and the right of
access to information.

This year marked a significant change in Council membership. I thank my predecessor,
the Hon Dr Brett Mason, who fulfilled the role of Chair with distinction from August 2018
to November 2024 and provided excellent advice to me as incoming Chair.

I also acknowledge former Chair of the Audit and Enterprise Risk Committee,
Doug Snedden AO, former Deputy Chair Janet Hirst, and Council member Alice Wong,
who all completed 9 years of service to Council in May 2025; former Chair of Council
Governance Committee Dr Bennie Ng who served for 6 years until April 2025; and
Richard Price, who served for 6 years until July 2025. I also thank outgoing parliamentary
members James Stevens and Senator Dr Jess Walsh for their contributions to the
National Library.

The last part of the year saw a smaller but dedicated Council group, with Dr Shane Simpson
AM reappointed for his third term, and 3 new appointees – Andrea Laing, Jane Hamilton
and Professor Sarah Holland-Batt FAHA FQA GAICD SFHEA taking up their new
responsibilities and learning – as I am still doing – about the breadth of the National
Library’s activities and the impact it creates for Australians wherever they live.

Last, I thank Dr Marie-Louise Ayres FAHA, her Executive team, the entire very talented
National Library workforce, and the National Library’s dedicated Friends and volunteers
for their passionate and professional pursuit of the National Library’s mission.




Distinguished Professor Larissa Behrendt AO FASSA FAHA FAAL




                                                                           Introduction 3
1.2 Director-General review
As the Chair has noted, the last year has seen us extend and deepen the value we add
for the broadest possible cross-section of the Australian community.

Building this impact requires deep capability across multiple dimensions:

 the buildings which protect collections and people

 the digital expertise and infrastructure that underpins so much of our value

 the strength of our relationships nationally and internationally

 a committed and highly skilled workforce.


The last year has seen the National Library continuing to care for and improve our built
environment. In May, we were finally able to remove the scaffolding which has ‘adorned’
the National Library’s iconic Parkes building for 5 years, with final roof remediation
complete, and 362 heritage windows replaced across levels 1 to 4 of the National Library.
The building again looks resplendent from the outside, while less visible works continue
within its walls.

Replacement of heating, ventilation, air-conditioning and associated safety systems
continued across 3 floors, with 60 staff working in leased premises, and a temporary
special collections reading room required for most of the year. We completed high risk
projects, with a successful move of the National Library’s data centre – the nerve centre
for Trove and the digital ecosystem – and construction and installation of the National
Library’s first new main switchboard since the 1960s.

The National Library’s collection continues to grow – both physically and digitally. This
year, we completed, fitted out and moved multiple collections across several floors to
maximise storage efficiency, from space and sustainability perspectives. Construction of
a much-needed extension to the National Library’s Hume repository also advanced apace,
and ahead of schedule – ready to house 18 linear kilometres of overseas collections
before the end of 2025.

Despite these disruptions, the Parkes building continued to welcome visitors from near and
far – to use the collections, enjoy events, visit our exhibitions, explore the best of Australian
writing in our bookshop, and of course to meet and converse in our popular cafes.

Equally important were significant upgrades to core digital systems, with a new, streamlined
and fully accessible website delivered, the introduction of Handwritten Text Recognition
starting to unlock the content of digitised manuscripts material, and replacement of a set
of interconnected systems on which the Australian library ecosystem relies.




4 NATIONAL LIBRARY OF AUSTRALIA                 ANNUAL REPORT 2024–25
These systems – which have supported libraries’ collection management and collection
sharing since the 1980s – make Australia’s one of the most efficient and connected library
systems in the world. Through deep engagement with our library partners, we were able
to further strengthen our relationships with and value to Australian libraries, in a year of
major change.

The National Library’s digital footprint is exceptionally large relative to organisation size,
and we have invested heavily to meet the challenges of ever-increasing cybersecurity
threats, protecting our collections and the personal data of those using our services.

The digital world is particularly fast-moving, with all organisations needing to invest more
heavily in defensive postures against these threats and engage with the possibilities and
the risks of the artificial intelligence (AI) revolution.

This year, the National Library was the first Australian cultural organisation to release
a fully considered AI Framework, developed in consultation with, and then shared
with, national and international stakeholders. The National Library’s focus is on further
‘unlocking’ the value in the national treasure that is the National Library’s collection.
The Framework mandates careful consideration of the legality, ethics, privacy and cultural
considerations of all potential projects.

Built and digital infrastructure are essential to our service to the Australian community
– but they rely on the knowledge, dedication, talent and passion of the National Library’s
workforce. That workforce is changing – it is younger, and with a much higher percentage of
staff who have joined the National Library in the last 5 years. The combination of the deep
knowledge developed by long-term staff, and the new skills and insights those joining more
recently bring, is proving both energising and fruitful in a fast-changing world.

Australia is incredibly well-served by these skilled professionals, all committed to
service to the Australian public, from east to west, north to south, city to the most
remote communities. They are a joy to work with, and Australia is fortunate to have
such dedicated and clever people meeting today’s needs and planning ambitiously
for the future.

We have benefited greatly from the guidance and insights of our Council. I offer
sincere thanks to the 2 Chairs serving the National Library over the last year –
the Hon Dr Brett Mason and Distinguished Professor Larissa Behrendt – and
all members of Council for their expertise and their ambition for what we can
achieve.




Dr Marie-Louise Ayres FAHA

2.1 Legislation
The National Library Act 1960 establishes the National Library and defines its role,
corporate governance and financial management framework. As a corporate
Commonwealth entity, the National Library is also governed by the Public Governance,
Performance and Accountability Act 2013 (the PGPA Act), which provides a system of
governance and accountability for public resources; and the Public Service Act 1999
(PS Act), which establishes our responsibility for efficiently and effectively staffing the
organisation in a way that serves the Australian Government and the public.



2.2 Our purpose
Under the National Library Act 1960, we collect and preserve documentary resources,
particularly relating to Australia and the Australian people, so Australians can discover,
learn and create new knowledge, now and in the future.



2.3 Our functions
Our functions, as defined in the National Library Act 1960, are to:

 maintain and develop a national collection of library material, including a comprehensive
  collection of library material relating to Australia and the Australian people

 make library material in the national collection available

 make available such other services in relation to library matters and library material as
  determined by the National Library Council

 cooperate in library matters with authorities or persons, whether in Australia or
  elsewhere, concerned with library matters.


This provides the strategic direction for all of our key actions and performance measures,
as outlined in the 2024–25 Corporate Plan.



2.4 Our collection
As Australia’s National Library, we prioritise collecting materials of national significance,
documenting the lives and activities of Australian people, places and events that have
shaped society. The collection spans manuscripts, maps, photographs, newspapers,
rare books and so much more.




8 NATIONAL LIBRARY OF AUSTRALIA                ANNUAL REPORT 2024–25
We are also stewards of rich archival, published, pictorial and oral history collections
documenting the experiences, culture and language of First Nations peoples – the First
Australians – and recognise the rights of First Nations peoples to be seen as primary
guardians, interpreters and decision-makers of their heritage.

In today’s digital world, we are proud to share an ever-increasing collection of stories and
research information online via Trove. Audiences can explore over 19 billion digital items
on Trove, accessible from anywhere in the world. It is free and available online all day,
every day.

3.2 First Nations
We recognise and respect the crucial place of First Nations stories
in Australia’s arts, culture and history.


STRATEGY: WHAT WE STRIVE FOR
We respect the rights of First Nations peoples as cultural authorities, primary guardians,
interpreters and decision-makers of their heritage. As a custodian of collections,
we pursue an Indigenous-led approach to managing culturally significant collections
and serving Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities and peoples.

The cornerstone of our approach is our Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property
(ICIP) protocol. Through all of our activities, we build relationships with people and
community.


OUTCOMES OF OUR 2024–25 ACTIONS

  Provide opportunity, space and time for Australia’s First Nations peoples to
  exercise self-determination by telling their own stories and interpreting their
  own histories and cultures through respectful dialogue, culturally appropriate
  events, activities, collection and description practice.

  We delivered a variety of Indigenous-led events over the past year. These events
  were important opportunities to welcome Indigenous peoples to the National Library,
  while inviting a broader audience to learn about Indigenous stories, history and culture.

  Our ‘In Conversation’ panel, Straight from the Strait, featured Luke Captain, Ruth Ghee,
  Georgia Corowa and facilitator Rhianna Patrick, who discussed their roles in producing
  a musical that commemorated the achievements of Torres Strait Islander workers
  and their families. Captain, Ghee and Corowa also recorded an oral history as part of
  their time at the National Library, adding traditional Torres Strait Islander songs to the
  collection.

  Our ‘Deadly Dialogues’ series continued with First Nations women in Science,

Modernise the underlying infrastructure to improve Trove efficiency
and capacity, including replacement of the metadata engine.

In 2024–25, we replaced the underlying infrastructure that supports the Australian
National Bibliographic Database (ANBD). The ANBD contains records for the more
than 35 million different items held in libraries, galleries, archives and museums
around Australia, and underpins both Trove and the inter-library loan and shared
cataloguing services it provides to member organisations. Implemented over 2 years,
the new Trove Metadata Manager system has significantly reduced running costs,
increased automation and provides more modern flexibility.

As part of the Trove Metadata Manager project, we adopted a linked data standard
called BIBFRAME for the newly designed and developed Trove Partners Cataloguing
tool. The cataloguing tool aligns with evolving international library practices and
includes a Trove extension, designed to capture Australia-specific metadata,
including Austlang codes for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander language material
and National edeposit (NED) information. In enhancing the cataloguing service, we
collaborated with Trove Partners, including establishing a consultative working group
to represent the library metadata community and understand the user experience.

The infrastructure renewal project supported modernised data harvesting, upgrading
to Open Archives Initiative Protocol for Metadata Harvesting. The result of this work
is that over 98% of Trove Partners now contribute their metadata daily via this secure
and automated process.

We also replaced the software underpinning national inter-library loan and document
delivery processes in May. The new software creates a more modern load-balancing
system and provides a more efficient and cost-effective solution.



In ongoing partnership with state and territory libraries, implement
high-priority enhancements to strengthen the National edeposit service.

We manage the NED service on behalf of Australia’s state, territory and national
libraries. The NED service provides for the deposit, management, storage,
preservation, discovery and delivery of published electronic material across Australia.
A total of 13,801 titles were deposited (excluding individual serial issues) with a total
of 95,253 items added (including serial issues) in 2024–25.

A major enhancement provides NED member libraries with a shared approach
to claiming new and missing issues of titles. This supports them in meeting
Commonwealth, state and territory legislative requirements to collect, preserve and
make assessable a comprehensive collection of Australia’s published output. This
enhancement provides the ability to efficiently identify and facilitate the deposit
and sequencing of issues of magazines, journals, newsletters and other recurrent
publications within the NED portal.

with 3 Trove Partners to tell the story of Beatlemania in Australia. Through the diverse
collections of the Royal Agricultural & Horticultural Society of South Australia,
the Powerhouse Museum (New South Wales) and the Arts Centre Melbourne (Victoria),
Trove audiences were invited to experience Beatlemania through the lens of earlier
generations.

The online collection of digitised newspapers and magazines are packed with images of the
huge crowds and the inventive tactics they used to get close to the ‘fab four’. It captures the
lengths the crowds would go to catch a glimpse of the legendary musicians on what was to
be one of their most popular tours of their career. The story is deeply enhanced by:

 the intimate letters, correspondence and financial records of the tour’s promoters
  held in the Arts Centre Melbourne’s collection

 the weird and wonderful memorabilia collected by the Powerhouse Museum, including
  chocolates and ‘The Beatles Urinal’, which was said to be used by the performers

 the thorough collecting of material covering media, promotion and venue hire
  (including restrictions on ‘stamping feet’) in the Royal Agricultural & Horticultural
  Society of South Australia, where half the population showed up to line the route
  from the airport to the city for the tour.


Bringing these collections together online gives a deeper understanding of the scale
and impact of events such as this tour and reveals often harder-to-find collections in
a significant national platform.

5.5 Risk management
    and fraud control
Our Enterprise Risk Management Framework promotes the continuous improvement of
risk culture and practice across the National Library. The Framework supports the Council,
the Audit and Enterprise Risk Committee and our Executive to effectively engage with
emerging risks and manage identified strategic and enterprise risks. The Framework also
provides support for business areas to identify, treat and manage operational risks.
The Framework aligns with the PGPA Act, the Commonwealth Risk Management Policy
2023 and ISO 31000:2018, Risk management—Guidelines.

We have zero tolerance for fraudulent behaviour and corruption. We treat both suspected
and actual fraud and corruption seriously. The Fraud and Corruption Control Plan was
updated to align with the new Commonwealth Fraud and Corruption Control Framework
and was endorsed by Council in November 2024. The plan sets out our strategies and
procedures for preventing, detecting and responding to fraud and corruption, in addition
to our comprehensive fraud and corruption risk assessment.



5.6 Business Continuity Committee
The Business Continuity Committee (BCC) is our principal committee for overseeing
the development, implementation and review of the Business Continuity Policy and
Framework, plan and emergency procedures.

Business continuity is an important component of sound corporate governance,
emergency preparedness and organisational resilience. We have adopted an approach
based on better practice models for cultural institutions to minimise disruptions that
affect our critical business processes.

We use an ‘all hazards’ approach, which recognises that different business continuity
events may cause similar issues, requiring similar actions and resources. This approach
empowers decision-makers to respond and adapt to a business continuity event as it
evolves, rather than attempting to predict events and predetermine possible actions.

The Business Continuity Policy and Framework was reviewed in August 2024. It outlines
our approach to planning and managing business disruptions, incidents and emergencies
that may occur. The Framework aligns with Managing disruption-related risk (AS/NZS 5050
(Int):2020) and the Australian Library & Information Association’s Disaster Management
Plan for Libraries (2019). The BCC has responsibility for implementing the Framework
and coordinating our crisis management responses.

The National Library recorded an accounting deficit of $200.851 million for the 2024–25
financial year compared to a surplus of $19.661 million in 2023–24. The deficit in 2024–25

acquisition of collection materials. Therefore, we are allowed to operate at a loss up to

Overview

Objectives of the National Library of Australia
The National Library of Australia (the Library) is an Australian Government controlled not-for-profit entity. The
Library’s role is to collect documentary resources, particularly relating to Australia and the Australian people, so
that the Australian community - now and in the future - can discover, learn and create new knowledge.

The Library has a single outcome: Enhanced learning, knowledge creation, enjoyment and understanding of
Australian life and society by providing access to a national collection of library material. The continued existence
of the Library in its present form and with its present programs is dependent on Government policy and on continuing
funding by parliament for the Library’s administration and programs.

Basis of Preparation
The financial statements are required by section 42 of the Public Governance, Performance and Accountability Act
2013.
The financial statements have been prepared in accordance with:
    a) Public Governance, Performance and Accountability (Financial Reporting) Rule 2015 (FRR); and
    b) Australian Accounting Standards and Interpretations – including simplified disclosures for Tier 2 Entities
         under AASB 1060 issued by the Australian Accounting Standards Board (AASB) that apply for the
         reporting period.

The financial statements have been prepared on an accrual basis and in accordance with the historical cost
convention, except for certain assets and liabilities at fair value. Except where stated, no allowance is made for the
effect of changing prices on the results or the financial position. The financial statements are prepared in Australian
dollars and rounded to the nearest thousand dollars.

Principles of Consolidation
The financial statements are those of the economic entity, comprising the Library (parent entity) and the Library's
Trust Accounts. The Library's Trust Accounts comprise funds originally received by way of gifts, bequests and
assignments. Eight of the nine Trust accounts have no beneficiaries with equitable interests. The Library derives
benefit in the use of these Trust assets consistent with the purposes of the individual trusts and objectives of the
Library and this enables the Library to meet its objectives of providing services within its functions prescribed by
the National Library Act 1960. Details of the Trust Accounts can be found at Note 6.2. The accounts of the Library's
Trust Accounts are prepared using accounting policies consistent with those of the Library. The effects of
transactions and balances between entities are eliminated in full.

The Library holds one Trust account which is not included within the financial statements of the Library as this
Trust has a beneficiary and is not deemed to be controlled by the Library. Details of this Trust account can be
found at Note 6.3.

Accounting Policy

Tangible Assets
Assets are recorded at cost on acquisition except as stated below. The cost of acquisition includes the fair value
of assets transferred in exchange and liabilities undertaken. Financial assets are initially measured at their fair
value plus transaction costs where appropriate.

Assets acquired at no cost, or for nominal consideration, are initially recognised as assets and income at their fair
value at the date of acquisition, unless acquired in a restructuring of administrative arrangements.

Asset Recognition Threshold
Purchases of property, plant and equipment are recognised initially at cost in the Statement of Financial Position,
except for purchases costing less than $5,000 (GST exclusive), which are expensed in the year of acquisition
(other than where they form part of a group of similar items which are significant in total). Purchases of library
materials are capitalised regardless of amount as part of the National Collection, which is a heritage and cultural
asset.

Lease Right of Use (ROU) Assets
Leased ROU assets are capitalised at the commencement date of the lease and comprise the initial lease liability
amount, initial direct costs incurred when entering into the lease less any lease incentives received. These assets
are accounted for by the Library as separate asset classes to corresponding assets owned outright, but included
in the same column as where the corresponding underlying assets would be presented if they were owned.

An impairment review is undertaken for any right of use lease asset that shows indicators of impairment and an
impairment loss is recognised against any right of use lease asset that is impaired. Leased ROU assets continue
to be measured at cost less accumulated depreciation and accumulated impairment losses after initial recognition.

Revaluations
Following initial recognition at cost, property and the National Collection are carried at fair value. It is the Library’s
policy to seek desktop valuation advice annually and full revaluation every five years to confirm all valuations
remain current.

Revaluation adjustments are made on a class basis. Any revaluation increment is credited to equity under the
heading of asset revaluation reserve except to the extent that it reverses a previous revaluation decrement of the
same asset class that was previously recognised in the surplus/deficit. Revaluation decrements for a class of
assets are recognised in the surplus/deficit except to the extent that they reverse a previous revaluation increment
for that class. Any accumulated depreciation as at the revaluation date is eliminated against the gross carrying
amount of the asset and the asset restated to the revalued amount.

Refer to Note 5.3 Fair Value Measurement for further details.

Depreciable property, plant and equipment are written-off in the Statement of Comprehensive income to their

useful life of the improvements or the unexpired period of the lease.

Depreciation/amortisation rates (useful lives), residual values and methods are reviewed at each reporting date
and necessary adjustments are recognised as appropriate.

Accounting Judgements and Estimates

Fair value of the National Collection
The Library obtains independent valuation advice annually of the National Collection through a desktop valuation
and every five years the independent valuers undertake a full revaluation (undertaken in 2024-25). At the end of
each desktop valuation the Library updates the assessment of fair value for the National Collection based on the
advice of the most recent independent valuation if the revaluation increment or decrement is considered material
to the financial statements. After the end of a full revaluation, the Library will update the fair value regardless of the
materiality to the financial statements.

Due to the quantities and diversity of the National Collection, the fair value measurement has been determined
using a stratified multistage sampling approach. Multistage sampling involves subdividing larger populations into
smaller, concentrated representative clusters or strata for valuation purposes. The process involves notionally
stratifying the low value items into several sub-groups, with a separate sample taken from each sub-group. The
aim of the stratification is to appropriately group items similar in nature and value. Stratification is necessary
because of the variability of the collections as each collection has its own characteristics and requires its own
sampling plan.

The valuation method adopted for the National Collection is the market approach. This approach provides an
indication of value by comparing the subject asset with similar assets for which price information is available. Under
this approach the first step is to consider the prices for transactions of similar assets that have occurred recently
in the market. If few recent transactions have occurred, it may also be appropriate to consider the prices of identical
or similar assets that are listed or offered for sale provided the relevance of this information is clearly established
and critically analysed. Market based evidence was sought from a range of sources including dealers in antiquarian
material, book sellers, specialist dealers and relevant sales databases.

The valuation of the National Collection is subject to a high level of estimation uncertainty. This uncertainty arises
from a number of factors including: many of the collection items are unique and there are few comparable
transactions; there is a limited market for most of the items in the collection; the Library does not sell assets and
therefore has no history of testing the market price; and the National Collection comprises many items and the
valuation process therefore depends on sampling, as explained above, and this gives rise to the risks inherent in
sampling.

These uncertainties mean that the valuation is an estimation process and that there may be significant variation
year on year in the overall valuation. To address the high level of estimation uncertainty the Library engages
professional qualified independent valuers to undertake desktop valuations annually and full valuations at least
every five years so that their carrying amount does not differ materially from the fair value at each reporting date.

In the 2024-25 financial year the Library refined its process used for determining the population for the valuation
of the collection and restructured certain strata into a greater level of homogeneous categorisations. The review
was a significant change to the stratification of the National Collection. The re-stratification of populations has
resulted in more consistent groupings, which is expected to significantly reduce volatility in the future. This
refinement in methodology has resulted in a material reduction in the fair value measurement of the National
Collection in 2024-25 but is expected to produce a more stable and reliable valuation outcome in the future.

Accounting Policy

Liabilities for short-term employee benefits and termination benefits expected within twelve months of the end of

Leave
The liability for employee benefits includes provision for annual leave and long service leave. The leave liabilities
are calculated on the basis of employees’ remuneration at the estimated salary rates that will be applied at the
time the leave is taken, including the Library’s employer superannuation contribution rates to the extent that the
leave is likely to be taken during service rather than paid out on termination.

The Library relies on a methodology developed by the Australian Government Actuary to estimate the present
value of a provision for annual and long service leave. The methodology for estimating the present value of the
long service leave uses probability factors for Library employees reaching unconditional entitlement and a
discount factor which provides for both interest effects and salary increases, both in terms of promotional salary
advancement and salary inflation. The Library management has reviewed the assessment at 30 June 2025 and
consider the value is reliable. The estimate of the present value of the liability takes into account attrition rates
and pay increases through promotion and inflation.

Provision is made for separation and redundancy benefit payments. The Library recognises a provision for
termination when it has developed a detailed formal plan for the terminations and has informed those employees
affected that it will carry out the terminations.

Superannuation
Employees of the Library are members of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme (CSS), the Public Sector
Superannuation Scheme (PSS), the PSS accumulation plan (PSSap) or other superannuation funds held outside
the Australian Government. The CSS and PSS are defined benefit schemes for the Australian Government. The
liability for the defined benefit schemes is recognised in the financial statements of the Australian Government
and will be settled by the Australian Government in due course. This liability is reported by the Department of
Finance’s administered schedules and notes.

The Library makes employer contributions to the employee's defined benefit superannuation scheme at rates
determined by an actuary to be sufficient to meet the current cost to the Australian Government. Employer
contributions to superannuation funds nominated by the employee are made at the same rate as those of the
PSSap. The Library accounts for the contributions as if they were contributions to defined contribution plans. The
liability for superannuation recognised as at 30 June represents outstanding contributions.

Accounting Policy

Financial assets
In accordance with AASB 9 Financial Instruments, the Library classifies its financial assets in the following
categories:
a) financial assets at fair value through profit and loss;
b) financial assets at fair value through other comprehensive income; and
c) financial assets measured at amortised cost.

The classification depends on both the entity's business model for managing the financial assets and contractual
cash flow characteristics at the time of initial recognition. Financial assets are recognised when the Library becomes
party to the contract and, as a consequence, has a legal right to receive or a legal obligation to pay cash and
derecognised when the contractual rights to the cash flows from the financial asset expire or are transferred upon
trade date.

Financial assets at amortised cost
Financial assets included in this category need to meet two criteria:
1. the financial asset is held in order to collect the contractual cash flows; and
2. the cash flows are solely payments of principal and interest on the principal outstanding amount.

Amortised cost is determined using the effective interest method.

Effective interest method
Income is recognised on an effective interest rate basis for financial assets that are recognised at amortised cost.

Financial liabilities
Financial liabilities are classified as either financial liabilities ‘at fair value through profit or loss’ or other financial
liabilities. Financial liabilities are recognised and derecognised upon ‘trade date’.

Financial liabilities at amortised cost
Financial liabilities, including borrowings, are initially measured at fair value, net of transaction costs. These
liabilities are subsequently measured at amortised cost using the effective interest method, with interest expense
recognised on an effective interest basis.

Supplier and other payables are recognised at amortised cost. Liabilities are recognised to the extent that the goods
or services have been received (and irrespective of having been invoiced).

AUSTRALIAN PUBLICATIONS, INCLUDING
LEGAL DEPOSIT
Notable Australian publication acquisitions during 2024–25 included:

Short Stories for the Mob by Taree writers, documenting contemporary realities,
experiences, and the history of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities.
The book highlights Indigenous life and connection to Country, cultural pride,
and resilience in ongoing challenges.

The economy of algorithms: AI and the rise of the digital minions by Marek Kowalkiewicz.
This title explores the significant impact of AI on various aspects of the economy.
Professor Marek Kowalkiewicz is a Polish–Australian computer scientist and Chair in Digital

transformation strategies for government and industry since 2019.

More of the Horrors of Transportation: Giving the full Particulars of the dreadful
condition of the Transports in Norfolk Island, Van Diemans’ Land, New South Wales,
&c. Broadside published in England c1839, the text is extracted from a lecture given
by a Mr McCarthy and recalls in gruesome detail the treatment and actions of convicts
in Macquarie Harbour, Norfolk Island and Phillip’s Island. It is a rare surviving example
of the broader social conditions and appetite in Britain for the Australian convict story.



AUSTRALIANA: MATERIAL BY AUSTRALIANS
AND ABOUT AUSTRALIANS
Notable Australiana themed acquisitions during 2024–25 included:

The Banksian Cockatoo [Calyyptorhynchus funereus, Yellow Tailed Black Cockatoo]
c1792 by Thomas Watling (1762–1814). Long considered to be the great convict artist,
Watling is thought to have painted this extraordinarily rare watercolour whilst at Sydney
Cove. Similarly rare is its subject, a bird that was not recorded in the collection of
Joseph Banks or early books about the colony (Phillip, White, Tench). One the very few
Watling artworks not held by the Natural History Museum, and relatively unknown to
research, this artwork will contribute to unravelling the debated story of first fleet art,
attribution and subject matter, in the earliest years of the British colony.

Papers of Susan Ryan – Trailblazing feminist, Labor politician and public servant.
She achieved several notable ‘firsts’, becoming the first female senator for the ACT
(1975–1988), the first woman in a Labor shadow cabinet (1977) and the first female
cabinet member in a federal Labor government (1983), going on to become Australia’s
first Age Discrimination Commissioner in 2011. Ryan ensured the Hawke government
ratified the 1980 United Nations Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of
Discrimination against Women, paving the way for the eventual passage of her Sex
Discrimination Bill 1984, which Ryan described as ‘probably the most useful thing I’ve
done in my life’. Ryan’s advocacy also influenced the Affirmative Action (Equal Employment
Opportunity for Women) Act 1986. Among select items donated are a recording of Ryan’s
maiden speech on entering Parliament (25 February 1976).

Papers of Bill Leak – Desmond Robert ‘Bill’ Leak (1956–2017) was a prominent Australian
editorial cartoonist and caricaturist. His archive spans the entire length of his career and
is an exceptional record of Australian political events and culture of the time. His cartoons
show the development of Leak’s idiosyncratic style from early black and white drawings,
through to the development of his distinctive and vivid use of colour. Leak’s cartoons
depict issues of national significance, often tapping into and exploring views from the
conservative perspective. In the latter years of his career, Leak’s work became the subject
of several controversies which had far-reaching impact.

Papers of Graeme Base – A first addition of 8 original artworks in watercolour and
transparent ink, associated with the publications Animalia, The Eleventh Hour, The Discovery
or Dragons, The Sign of the Seahorse, My Grandma Lived in Gooligulch, The Worst Band in the
Universe, Enigma and the Legend of the Golden Snail.

Papers of Dr Helen Brittain – Lifelong friend and correspondent with Colleen
McCullough, following their time at Yale University, and to whom McCullough dedicated
her first thriller. Included in these papers are an early draft of The Thorn Birds and letters
from McCullough to Brittain describing its drafting, as well as the writing process on

Our notable acquisitions during 2024–25 included:

YARABINDJA BUDJURUNG I and II, 2021, a map by Megan Cope documenting the
relationships First Nations and non-Indigenous Australians have with the environment
through an engagement with Land Rights, Deep Time, memory and place names. In the
Quandamooka language Jandai, the title translates as ‘beautiful sea country’. Showing
Cope’s ancestral Country, Indigenous place names are hand-inscribed in 19th century
typeface across the map. Cope describes these works as ‘a conceptual documentation
of Country’, and Cope’s cartographic work has more generally been understood as

Mabo Claim I, 2022 by Gail Mabo, Townsville-based multidisciplinary artist and writer,
whose work often discusses Torres Strait Islander Knowledge, political history and
advocacy and her own family history, as daughter of Edward Koiki Mabo. Mabo Claim I is
created by Gail Mabo to reflect upon her father’s leadership and guidance, combining the
iconography of her father’s maps with the tradition of navigational stick charts. Stick charts
were used in Micronesia and the Torres Strait for navigation by boat, although they were
not usually taken onboard; rather, they were memorised by the voyager.

Photographs of the inaugural First Nations Netball Tournament by Rachel Bach.

15 September 2024 at the State Netball Centre in Melbourne. Under-23 teams from every
state and territory in Australia competed across 3 days of competition, aiming to provide
opportunities for First Nations participants to showcase the talent of those taking part.

Notable acquisitions during 2024–25 included:

Correspondence of Christian Rümker, 1844–1865, Australia’s first Government
Astronomer (1827–1830), with European astronomers such as John Lee, and Rümker’s
son, Georg Rümker. Christian Rümker was an important astronomer who significantly
advanced the mapping of the Southern Hemisphere skies while working at the Paramatta
Observatory in New South Wales, between 1821 and 1828. He created a catalogue of
about 7,000 stars which greatly improved the accuracy of astronomical observations in
this region.

Stereoscopic photograph, A peep down the Great Melbourne Telescope c1870,
presenting a 3-dimensional ‘peep’ down the telescope, represents a significant
development in Australia’s scientific capabilities. At the time, it was the second largest
telescope in the world and was proof of ‘Melbourne’s claim to being the leading
metropolis in the Southern Hemisphere’ during its Gold Rush-era boom. The telescope
was designed to look at nebulae in the southern skies.

Photographic series, Research into the Tasmanian Tiger, 2024, by Dave Tacon, offers
an important insight into groundbreaking research at the University of Melbourne Tigrr
Lab where scientists are seeking to genetically restore Thylacinus cynocephalus, also known
as the Tasmanian tiger. The Tasmanian tiger became extinct in 1936 as a direct result of
human influence after the government-imposed bounty for its perceived destruction on
livestock. The de-extinction project has produced a thylacine genome and aims to create
a complete specimen using living cells and genomic template from its closest relative, the
fat-tailed dunnart.

Professor Michelle Haber AO, interviewed by Daniel Connell for the oral history
Eminent stream. Professor Haber was appointed a Member of the Order of Australia
for her services to science in the field of research into childhood cancer, to scientific
education and to the community in 2007. She holds a conjoint appointment as Professor
in the Faculty of Medicine at UNSW Australia and has been Executive Director at the
Children’s Cancer Institute since 2003.

Dick Telford a sports scientist, distance running coach and former Australian rules
football player, interviewed by Nikki Henningham for the oral history Eminent stream.
Mr Telford played for Collingwood and Fitzroy in the Victorian Football League in the
1960s. He was the first sports scientist to be employed by the Australian Institute of
Sport (AIS), and coached marathon runner Robert de Castella.

and town, and particularly the 2 major rivers that flow through Warrnambool, Victoria,

of the mite since its arrival in Australia in 2022, have become a central focus of the
industry. This series shows the efforts of apiarists to protect their hives, and of workers
for the NSW Department of Primary Industries to assist and educate in prevention and
treatment of the mite.

Notable acquisitions during 2024–25 included:

‘The Printing of Books’ [Impressio librorum], fourth plate from New Inventions of
Modern Times, by Philips Galle (engraver and publisher, 1537–1612) after
Jan van der Straet. This engraving is one of the most important and iconic depictions
of a late 16th-century European printing workshop. It depicts the history and technology
of the book, showing how the National Library’s rare books were made.

Two manuscript maps, and a pamphlet showing the Santa Cruz Islands (southern
Solomon Islands, near Vanuatu), c1800 by Charles Francois Beautemps-Beaupre.
The manuscript maps preserve calculations of the first large-scale scientific expedition
to systematically and faithfully derive latitude and longitude from precise astronomical
observations while also plotting through calculating angular distances between the sun
and coastal landmarks. This, in conjunction with the manuscript pamphlet, and the
vendor’s statement that the printed chart on offer could pre-date the 1807 atlas held
by the National Library, suggests the rich research potential of this discrete cartographic
collection for the history of cartography as well as the history of exploration of the
South Pacific.
